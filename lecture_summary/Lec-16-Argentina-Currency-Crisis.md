# Lecture 16: Argentina Currency Crisis
## A Long History of Financial Crises (1975–2025)

---

## 1. The Rodrigazo Crisis (1975)

### What Happened
In 1975, Argentina's military government (led by Isabel Perón, Perón's widow) faced a fiscal crisis. The austral currency collapsed.

### The Devaluation
| Item | Change |
|------|--------|
| **Currency** | Devalued 150% |
| **Fuel prices** | +180% |
| **Utilities** | +100% |
| **Wages** | +45% |

### The Consequences
- **Riots** over price spikes
- **Political instability** → military coup in 1976
- **FX reserves drained**: ~$1.2 billion in unrepaid loans to Cuba

### The Lesson
Currency devaluation with inadequate wage support = social unrest.

---

## 2. Hyperinflation & the Primavera Plan (1989)

### The Inflation Spiral
**Inflation hit 3,000%+ per year** (monthly inflation >100%).

### The Failed Stabilization: Primavera Plan (1988)
Argentina tried a **heterodox shock**: unconventional anti-inflation program combining:
- **Price pacts**: Government agreements with business groups (UIA, Chamber of Commerce) to voluntarily limit monthly price increases in exchange for VAT tax cuts (18% → 15%)
- **Multi-tier exchange rates**: Different rates for different transaction types
- **Austerity**: Spending cuts

### Why It Failed
- **FX reserves ran out**: Central bank burned ~$900 million defending the austral peg
- **Capital flight**: Investors moved money out
- **Price controls collapsed**: Once reserves exhausted, prices broke free

### The Outcome
- Peg collapsed; pesification (peso) and **dollarization** (people switched to USD)
- President Alfonsín forced to resign early
- Lesson: Can't fix inflation with price controls alone if reserves aren't there to back the currency

---

## 3. The Convertibility Plan (1991)

### A New Experiment: Currency Board
President Carlos Menem (1989–1999) tried a radical fix.

**Convertibility Plan (1991)**: A **currency board** pegging the peso 1:1 to the US dollar.

### How a Currency Board Works
- **Fixed peg**: Peso = 1 USD, backed by actual USD reserves
- **No printing**: Central bank cannot finance government deficits by printing money
- **Discipline**: Forces fiscal and monetary discipline
- **Credibility**: Fixed exchange rate locks in price stability

### Initial Success (1991–1998)
- **Inflation tamed**: From 1,000%+ to single digits
- **Growth**: GDP grew 7–8% annually
- **Dollarization advantage**: Cheap credit, confidence returns
- **Downside**: Exports became expensive; manufacturing lagged

## 4. The Great Depression (1998–2002)

### External Shocks Hit
- **Asian crisis** (1997–98): Global contagion
- **Russian default** (1998): Capital flight from all EMs
- **Brazilian crisis** (1998–99): Regional domino
- **Drought** (1999–2002): Agricultural output collapsed

### Argentina's Dilemma
With peso pegged 1:1 to USD:
- **Can't devalue** (would break the peg)
- **Can't print money** (currency board prohibits it)
- **Trapped**: Real exchange rate became overvalued (peso too expensive)

### The Collapse (2001–2002)

#### Political Instability
- Four presidents in two weeks (December 2001)
- Social unrest; riots over bank freezes

#### The Peg Breaks (January 2002)
- Peso suddenly floats
- **Falls from 1:1 to ~4:1 USD/ARS**
- Currency collapsed 75%

#### Economic Devastation
| Indicator | Impact |
|-----------|--------|
| **GDP** | Fell 28% |
| **Unemployment** | Roughly doubled |
| **Poverty** | Roughly doubled |
| **Default** | $95 billion—largest sovereign default at the time |

#### The Corralito (Bank Freeze)
- **Government froze bank withdrawals** to prevent bank collapse
- People couldn't access their deposits for months
- Trust in financial system destroyed

### The Lesson: Currency Boards Work Until They Don't
- Fixed pegs are credible when you can defend them
- Once fundamentals deteriorate too much, they break catastrophically
- Break = massive devaluation, defaults, unemployment

## 5. Recovery and the RUFO Trap (2002–2014)

### Post-2002 Recovery
After the peso floated:
- **Exports became competitive** (weak peso helped manufacturing)
- **GDP growth resumed** (~7% annually 2003–2008)
- **Default restructuring**: Creditors accepted 25–30 cent on the dollar (2005)

### The 2014 Technical Default: RUFO Clause Trap

#### Background: Holdout Creditors
- Some creditors (esp. hedge funds, "vulture funds") rejected the 2005 restructuring
- They sued to recover 100% of their claims
- Judge Griesa (US court) sided with them

#### The Pari Passu Ruling
**Pari passu** (Latin: "equal treatment"): If some bondholders get paid, all must get paid equally.

**The trap**: If Argentina paid holdouts 100%, the **RUFO Clause** (Rights Upon Future Offers) would trigger.

#### The RUFO Clause Explained
Argentina's restructured debt contracts included: **"If others get a better offer, we get the same."**

**The impossible math**:
- If Argentina paid holdouts 100%, RUFO-compliant bondholders demanded 100% too
- Total payoff: ~€100 billion
- Argentina couldn't afford it
- **Selective default**: Paid compliant creditors; didn't pay holdouts
- Resolved in 2016 with a compromise

### The Lesson: Contract Language Matters
Debt restructuring needs **clean exit consents** (clear legal closure) or vulture funds can extract billions.

## 6. Recurrent Crises (2013–2025): The Peso Under Pressure

### Why Another Crisis?
- **Fed normalization** (2013–2015): Taper tantrum hits EMs
- **Commodity bust**: Oil, agricultural prices fell
- **Capital outflows**: EM investors fled
- **Overvaluation**: Peso became too strong (hard to export)

### Multiple Devaluations
- **2013–14 devaluation**: ~35%
- **2018 crisis**: Record **$57 billion IMF bailout** (largest ever for Argentina)
- **2020 debt restructuring**: Another round with creditors
- **2023 Milei devaluation**: **54% deliberate devaluation** to reset the currency

### The Pattern: Boom → Overvaluation → Crisis → Devaluation → Repeat

---

## 7. The Milei Shock (2023–2025)

### The New President: Javier Milei
Elected in November 2023 on a radical reform platform.

### Deliberate Devaluation Strategy
- **2023 devaluation**: 54% (deliberate, not chaotic)
- **Purpose**: Correct overvaluation; make exports competitive
- **Painful but planned**: Unlike previous crises, this was intentional

### IMF Extended Fund Facility (April 2025)
Milei entered a new IMF program with strict conditionality:
- **Goal**: Control inflation (50%+ annually)
- **Goal**: Rebuild FX reserves
- **Mechanism**: Fiscal discipline + gradual currency stabilization

## 8. Understanding IMF Program Conditionality

### IMF Programs: SBA, EFF, Standby Arrangements

#### 2018 SBA (Stand-By Arrangement)
Short-term emergency financing

#### 2022 EFF (Extended Fund Facility)
Longer-term program, multi-year conditions

#### 2025–26 EFF
Milei's current program

### Types of Conditions

#### QPC (Quantitative Performance Criteria)
**Definition**: Numerical targets the country must hit.

**Examples**:
- **Primary fiscal surplus**: Must reach 1.4–2.5% of GDP (stop spending more than you earn)
- **Monetary financing ceiling**: Central bank cannot finance government deficit by printing money
- **Payment arrears ceiling**: Limit on overdue payments to creditors

#### NIR Floors (Net International Reserves)
**Definition**: Minimum foreign currency reserves the central bank must hold.

**Example**: "Must maintain at least $15 billion in reserves at all times"

**Purpose**: Ensures country has buffers to defend currency if crisis hits

## 9. Understanding IMF Financing: SDRs

### What Are SDRs?
**Special Drawing Right**: IMF's reserve asset, a composite of major world currencies.

### SDR Composition
| Currency | Weight |
|----------|--------|
| USD | 41.73% |
| EUR | 30.93% |
| CNY | 10.92% |
| JPY | 8.33% |
| GBP | 8.09% |

SDR value = weighted average of these 5 currencies.

### How SDR Borrowing Works
1. **Country borrows SDRs** from IMF (during crisis)
2. **Interest owed**: Pays interest to the country from which SDRs originally came
3. **Conversion**: Converts SDRs to actual USD/EUR/etc. for use
4. **Repayment**: Repays SDRs + interest later (usually 3.25–5 years)

### Key Point: SDRs ≠ Regular Money
- **Only IMF members** can borrow SDRs (not individuals, not banks)
- Used exclusively for **official crisis financing**
- Not used in everyday transactions
- Acts as a common pool of reserves for the system

## 10. Argentina's Pattern: The Structural Vulnerability

### The Recurring Problem
Argentina keeps hitting the same wall:
1. Strong commodity exports (grain, beef, oil)
2. Price booms → fiscal spending increases
3. Central bank prints money → inflation
4. Currency becomes overvalued
5. Exports suffer; imports booming
6. Foreign reserves drain; capital flight
7. Crisis → devaluation/default
8. Repeat

### Why It's Hard to Break
- **Political incentives**: Easier to spend and print than raise taxes (unpopular)
- **Institutional weakness**: Tax collection weak; fiscal discipline poor
- **Commodity dependence**: Boom-bust cycles outside government control
- **Dollarization**: People and firms use USD, making devaluation painful

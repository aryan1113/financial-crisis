# Lecture 11: The Global Financial Crisis (GFC), 2007–09
## Origins, Unfolding, and Policy Response

---

## 1. Overview

### Scale and Impact
- **Timing**: Mid-2007 to mid-2009
- **Origin**: US sub-prime mortgage crisis
- **Global spread**: Triggered the Great Recession—the worst downturn since 1929–39
- **Epicenter**: United States

---

## 2. Regulatory Foundations of the Boom (1992–2004)

### A Timeline of Deregulation

| Year | Policy | Impact |
|------|--------|--------|
| **1992** | Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac affordable-housing mandates | Increased demand for sub-prime mortgages |
| **1995** | Community Reinvestment Act changes | Fueled sub-prime lending expansion |
| **1999** | Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act repealed Glass-Steagall | Removed wall between commercial and investment banking |
| **2000** | Commodity Futures Modernization Act | Deregulated credit default swaps (CDS) |
| **2004** | SEC relaxed net capital rule (12:1 → ~30:1) | Investment banks could leverage 30x |

---

## 3. Sub-Prime Lending: The Core Problem

### Who Got Sub-Prime Loans?
**FICO Credit Score Ranges:**
- 800–850: Excellent
- 740–799: Very Good
- 670–739: Good
- **580–669: Fair/Sub-prime (higher risk)**
- 300–579: Poor (high risk)

Sub-prime lending targeted the 580–669 range at above-prime interest rates (higher than standard loans).

### Why It Mattered
- **Predatory lending**: Loans to borrowers who couldn't reliably repay
- **Assumption**: Housing prices always go up, so borrowers could refinance
- **Reality**: Housing prices fell, trapping borrowers with unaffordable mortgages

---

## 4. The Securitization Chain: How Risk Got Hidden

### Step 1: Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS)
- **Definition**: A financial product whose price is based on underlying mortgages
- **Mechanism**: Banks bundled mortgages and sold them to investors, transferring risk from bank → investor
- **Cash flow**: Mortgage payments from homeowners passed through to investors

### Step 2: Collateralized Debt Obligations (CDO)
- **What**: A repackaging structure bundling payments from both prime (reliable) and sub-prime (risky) debt
- **The trick**: Divided into "tranches" (layers) of different risk levels
  - **Lower tranches**: Absorbed defaults first (high risk, high return)
  - **Senior tranches**: Protected from defaults until lower layers exhausted (lower risk, lower return)
- **The problem**: This structure masked the underlying credit risk—investors in senior tranches thought they were safe when they weren't

### Step 3: Credit Insurance That Didn't Work
- **CDS (Credit Default Swaps)**: Insurance-like contracts on mortgage bonds
- **Assumption**: If bond defaults, CDS seller pays—so bond holders are protected
- **The catch**: CDS sellers (like AIG) didn't have enough capital to pay claims when crisis hit

### Step 4: Short-Term Funding (Asset-Backed Commercial Paper, ABCP)
- Used to fund illiquid mortgage assets
- **Froze in August 2007**: Investors stopped rolling over short-term debt, cutting off funding

## 5. Tracking the Crisis: Key Risk Indicators

### What These Measure
These metrics show when credit conditions are tightening and fear is rising:

| Indicator | Measures | What It Shows |
|-----------|----------|---------------|
| **TED Spread** | 3-month LIBOR minus 3-month Treasury | Bank-to-bank credit risk (widens in crises) |
| **Baa Spread** | Moody's Baa corporate bonds minus 10-year Treasury | Corporate credit risk for medium-quality firms |
| **VIX** | 30-day expected volatility of S&P 500 index | Equity market fear |
| **MOVE** | Merrill Lynch bond option volatility index | Treasury market fear (the "VIX for bonds") |

## 6. Crisis Timeline

### 2007: Early Cracks
- **April 2007**: New Century Financial (sub-prime lender) collapses
- **Sept 2007**: Northern Rock (UK bank) faces bank run

### 2008: Collapse
- **March 2008**: Bear Stearns rescued by JPMorgan (Fed-arranged)
- **Sept 7–10, 2008**: US government seizes Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac
  - These two lenders held/guaranteed nearly half of US mortgages
  - Government invoked "systemic risk" justification
  - Treasury determined their debt posed catastrophic contagion risk
- **Sept 15, 2008**: Lehman Brothers declares bankruptcy (largest US bank failure)
- **Sept-Oct 2008**: AIG bailout, Washington Mutual failure
- **Global spillover**: Stock market crashes worldwide, runs on banks

### 2009: Central Bank Response
- Liquidity injections
- Aggressive rate cuts (Fed funds near zero)

## 7. Policy Response

### Government Intervention Programs

#### TARP (Troubled Asset Relief Program)
- **Size**: $700 billion authorized (later $475 billion spent)
- **Purpose**: Purchase mortgage-backed securities and bank equity stakes
- **Mechanism**: Inject capital directly into distressed financial institutions

#### New Regulatory Agencies & Rules

| Program | Purpose |
|---------|---------|
| **FSOC** (Financial Stability Oversight Council) | Identify financial fragilities and coordinate regulation across agencies |
| **CFPB** (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau) | Regulate consumer financial products and prevent predatory lending |
| **Volcker Rule** | Restrict banks from speculative proprietary trading; limit hedge fund/PE investments |
| **Stress Tests** | Mandate regular stress-testing of banks and increase capital requirements |

---

## 8. Key Lessons

### What Went Wrong
- **Weak regulation**: Allowed overleveraged banks and predatory lending
- **Misaligned incentives**: Lenders didn't bear the risk—they sold loans immediately
- **Asset bubbles**: Housing bubble wasn't tracked or stopped early
- **Systemic risk**: "Too Big to Fail" created moral hazard—banks took excessive risk knowing government would bail them out

### What's Needed Going Forward
- **Stronger oversight** of credit growth and asset bubbles
- **Aligned incentives**: Lenders should retain some risk
- **Global coordination**: Crises spread internationally; regulation needs cross-border coordination
- **Robust emergency tools**: Central banks need clear "Lender of Last Resort" frameworks and bailout procedures

# Lecture 10: The Asian Financial Crisis (1997–98)

---

## 1. The East Asian Miracle (Before the Crisis)

### The Setup
Between 1987–96, East Asia achieved extraordinary growth:
- **ASEAN-5**: Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Thailand, Singapore (grew ~7.9%/year)
- **Plus**: Korea, Hong Kong, Taiwan

### What Drove the Boom
- Trade liberalization and deregulation
- Competent bureaucracies (though often authoritarian)
- Growth-oriented industrial policies
- Strong export-promotion institutions

**The assumption**: This region was on an unstoppable growth trajectory.

---

## 2. The Trigger: Thailand (July 1997)

### What Happened
On **July 2, 1997**, Thailand's central bank (Bank of Thailand) exhausted its foreign exchange reserves trying to defend the USD peg. Investors had been shorting the Thai baht, and the bank couldn't hold the line.

### The Result
- Thailand **floated the baht** (stopped defending the peg)
- The currency collapsed
- **Nickname**: "Tom Yam Kung" crisis (named after a Thai soup)—reflecting the messy mix of currency and banking problems

---

## 3. Contagion: How It Spread

### Timeline of the Collapse

**Initial spread (weeks):**
Thailand → Philippines → Malaysia → Indonesia → Korea → Hong Kong → global spillovers

**Global spillovers:**
- Japan (financial stress)
- Russia (ruble default, August 1998)
- Brazil (capital flight)
- United States (**LTCM collapse**: Federal Reserve organized a $3.5 billion bailout consortium for Long-Term Capital Management, the largest US hedge fund at the time, as its failure threatened to take down top US banks)

### What is Contagion?
**Definition**: A financial crisis spreading like wildfire across countries and markets, even to places with no direct exposure to the original problem.

### IMF Bailout Packages
- **Thailand**: $17 billion
- **Indonesia**: $40 billion (later revised upward)
- **Korea**: $57 billion
- **Plus**: Russia and Brazil packages

---

## 4. Political Fallout

### Indonesia
- President Suharto resigned in May 1998
- Trigger: Riots over subsidy cuts and the rupiah's collapse (from ~2,500 to 16,800 per USD)

### Korea
- Change in government
- Painful labor market reforms

### Russia
- Multiple Prime Minister changes
- Ruble crisis management

---

## 5. Root Causes: Why It Happened

### The Vulnerabilities
- **Rising foreign debt**: ASEAN-4 countries saw foreign debt/GDP ratio jump from 100% to 180%
- **Asset bubbles**: Property and stock markets inflated far beyond fundamentals
- **External deficits**: Countries were spending more than they earned
- **Pegged exchange rates**: Created an implicit government guarantee that encouraged companies to borrow in foreign currency without hedging
- **Weak regulation**: Poor prudential oversight and directed lending (loans to politically connected firms)
- **Lack of transparency**: Poor data about currency reserves, debt, and financial sector health
- **Governance uncertainty**: Political risk made investors nervous

### The Chain Reaction
When investors realized growth wasn't sustainable and tried to pull money out, the peg couldn't hold.

---

## 6. Different Responses: The Malaysia Experiment

### Malaysia's Unorthodox Path
Prime Minister Mahathir rejected the standard IMF playbook:
- Instead of **austerity** (cutting spending), he imposed **capital controls** (restricting money flows out of the country)
- Instead of floating the ringgit, he **pegged it again**

### The Outcome
- **Controversial** at the time (many economists said it would fail)
- **Faster recovery** than neighboring countries using IMF orthodoxy
- **Political victory** for Mahathir

---

## 7. Structural Lessons

### Key Vulnerability Indicators
- **Large short-term foreign debt** relative to forex reserves = danger zone
- **Rapid credit growth** + weak supervision = crisis amplification
- **Open capital accounts** (free money flows) increase crisis risk

### The Trilemma (Impossible Trinity)
A country cannot have all three of these simultaneously:
1. **Independent monetary policy** (ability to set interest rates independently)
2. **Full capital account convertibility** (free flow of money across borders)
3. **Fixed or managed exchange rate** (currency stability)

**Countries must choose which two to prioritize.**

### The "Dirty Float" Compromise
Many countries use a **"dirty float"** strategy: defending the currency sometimes while preserving forex reserves other times, rather than committing to either pure float or pure fix.

### Capital Controls
- Offer only temporary relief
- Come at a reputational cost (makes investors nervous about investing in that country long-term)
- More defensible in crises than as permanent policy

## Learning Level

**Conceptual/narrative** case history: how an unsustainable peg triggers a currency crisis that cascades regionally and globally, interwoven with politics.

**Background needed**: Basic macroeconomics (money supply, exchange rates, inflation, forex reserves).

**Quantitative content**: Foreign debt/GDP ratios, reserve-coverage ratios, credit growth, exchange rates—illustrative, not analytical. No mathematical models.

**Core intuition**: Peg defended with limited reserves → speculative attack → forced float → debt/banking stress → political crisis

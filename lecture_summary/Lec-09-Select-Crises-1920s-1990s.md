# Select Financial Crises: 1920s–1990s
## Great Depression, Latin American Debt, ERM, and Tequila Crisis

---

## 1. Great Depression (1929–39)

### What Happened
The 1920s "Roaring Twenties" stock bubble burst on **Black Thursday/Monday/Tuesday** (October 24/28/29, 1929). The Dow fell roughly 90% by 1932. Industrial production, prices, and trade collapsed across the US, UK, France, and Germany. US unemployment reached 24.9%.

### Two Key Interpretations

**Friedman & Schwartz blamed the Federal Reserve:**
- Tightened in 1928
- Raised rates in 1931 to defend the dollar
- Raised again in late 1932
- Failed to act as "Lender of Last Resort" during bank failures
- Result: monetary contraction deepened the crisis

**Eichengreen blamed the gold standard:**
- The gold standard forced rate hikes to defend gold parity
- This prevented the Fed from easing the economy even though it needed it
- Similar dynamic later appeared in the 2008-09 euro crisis debate

## 2. Latin American Debt Crisis (1980s)

### The Build-up
Oil shocks in the 1970s generated large surpluses in oil-exporting countries. US banks recycled these surpluses into loans to Latin America. Debt ballooned from $29 billion (1970) to $327 billion.

### The Trigger
In 1979-80, Federal Reserve Chair Paul Volcker raised rates aggressively to fight inflation (Fed funds rate hit 20%). This triggered:
- Capital flight from Latin America
- Mexico's 1982 default (the turning point)

### The Resolution: Brady Plan (1989)
The **Brady Plan** used debt-for-bond restructuring and forgave about $60 billion in debt. This allowed countries to rebuild creditworthiness and re-enter capital markets.

### Domestic Anti-Inflation Attempts (Heterodox Shock Programs)
Countries like Argentina, Bolivia, and Brazil tried unconventional approaches to combat "inertial" inflation (wage and price freezes linked to past inflation):
- **Brazil's attempts**: Cruzado, Bresser, Summer, and Collor Plans all failed
- **Brazil's success**: The 1994 **Plano Real** finally tamed hyperinflation using a transitional currency unit (URV) and gradual de-indexation
- **Brazil's reversal**: Fiscal slippage led to the 1999 "Samba Effect" currency crisis, worsened by Russian/Asian contagion and defensive rate hikes

### Key Innovation: Brady Bonds
- **What**: 25-30 year USD bonds collateralized by zero-coupon US Treasury bonds
- **Why it mattered**: The US Treasury collateral backing made restructured debt credible to international investors
- **Impact**: Crisis-hit countries could refinance and re-enter capital markets with restored creditworthiness

## 3. European ERM Crisis (1992–93)

### What is the ERM?
The **European Exchange Rate Mechanism** (established 1979) was a fixed-peg system designed to keep European currencies stable. It required member countries to meet strict **Maastricht convergence criteria**: limits on inflation, deficit/debt ratios, exchange-rate stability, and interest rates.

### Black Wednesday (September 16, 1992)
- **George Soros's Quantum Fund** shorted sterling (bet the pound would fall)
- **Bank of England** tried to defend the peg with foreign exchange intervention—and failed
- **UK exited the ERM** under pressure
- **Domino effect**: Lira, peseta, escudo, and other currencies came under speculative pressure and fell

## 4. Mexican Tequila Crisis (1994–95)

### The Setup
Mexico defended a peso peg using **Tesabonos**—peso-denominated debt with implicit dollar guarantees. This created the illusion of a safe, stable currency.

### Political Shocks Raised Risk
- Chiapas uprising (indigenous rebellion)
- Assassination of presidential candidate Luis Donaldo Colosio
- Result: risk premium on Mexican debt spiked

### Reserve Depletion & Crisis
- Mexico's foreign exchange reserves drained as investors lost confidence
- December 1994: Unable to defend the peg, Mexico devalued and floated the peso
- **Capital flight followed**: 52% inflation, deep recession
- **Bailout**: US and IMF provided $50 billion in emergency support

### The Broader Lesson: "Original Sin"
**Definition**: Emerging markets' inability to borrow abroad in their own currency—they must borrow in foreign currencies (USD, GBP, EUR, JPY) instead.

Mexico tried to work around this with Tesabonos (peso debt with dollar guarantees), but when the peso collapsed, the dollar-guarantee mechanism failed. This crisis showed the persistence of "original sin" as a structural vulnerability.

## Relevance to the Crisis Simulation

**Rating: High**

Both the **Tequila crisis** and **ERM crisis** are near-direct templates for the simulation:
- **Tequila**: peg defense → reserve depletion → forced devaluation → IMF bailout
- **ERM**: central bank vs. speculators → currency defense failure → exit from peg

These provide concrete playbooks for decision-making in RBI Governor, Finance Ministry, IMF Mission Chief, and Fed Chair roles.

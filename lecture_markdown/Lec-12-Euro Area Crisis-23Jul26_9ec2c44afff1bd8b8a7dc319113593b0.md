What was the Euro area Crisis of 2009-12?

2

▪ The Euro area crisis, also known as the Eurozone (EZ) crisis was a multi-year

debt crisis that started in 2009 and lingered till 2012.

▪ The  crisis first manifested in Greece but then engulfed several countries in
euro area, especially Cyprus and other PIGS (Portugal, Italy, Greece and
Spain) countries as they were unable to refinance their public debt and faced
sovereign defaults. The acronym soon changed from PIGS to PIIGS after
Ireland joined the grouping of EZ countries facing sovereign debt problems.

▪ These countries faced BOP crisis with sudden stop of capital inflows, but the
root of the crisis lay in a “diabolic loop”, also called the “doom loop”,
between sovereign debt and bank debt with bank bailouts leading to
unsustainable public debt.

What was the Euro area Crisis of 2009-12?

3

▪ Greece crisis opened up two possibilities – (i) Greece being taken out of euro
area in an orderly fashion; (ii) Greece being bailed out through financial
support. The latter was chosen as EU feared that Greece exiting euro area could
cause contagion that will result in a collapse of euro.

▪ Greece received three EU/ESM)–IMF bailout packages that were worked out by
the the "Troika" (European Commission, European Central Bank, and IMF).

▪ First Bailout (2010): A €110 billion package. Ireland and Portugal received EU-
IMF bailouts In November 2010 and May 2011, respectively. In March 2012,
Greece received its second bailout. Both Spain and Cyprus received rescue
packages in June 2012 and banks were bailed out in March 2013.

▪ Second Bailout (2012): A €130 billion package backed by an IMF Extended

Fund Facility (EFF) and private bondholder debt restructuring ("haircut"). In March
2012, Greece received its second bailout. Both Spain and Cyprus received
rescue packages in June 2012 and some banks were bailed out in March 2013.

▪ Third Bailout (2015): Up to €86 billion provided through the European Stability

Mechanism (ESM) after intense political negotiations.

▪ EZ crisis manifested itself as a BOP crisis due to sudden stop of capital flows to the

Southern periphery countries, but had its roots in bank bailouts

▪ EZ crisis had a major political impact on the ruling governments and in 10 out
of 19 EZ countries there was a power shift (in Greece, Ireland, France, Italy,
Portugal, Spain, Slovenia, Slovakia, Belgium, and the Netherlands; as also in the UK
which was outside the EZ).

▪ A European Financial Stability Facility (ESFS) was also created in June 2010 as a
temporary fiscal backstop and it lent support to Ireland, Portugal & Greece through
ESFS bonds and other debt instruments. In September 2012, the ESFS was
converted into a permanent Regional Financial Arrangement (RFA) called the
European Stability Mechanism (ESM) - a facility with € 750 bn in firepower and
disbursed loans of €295 bn.

▪ The bailouts by Troika have been criticized for excessive austerity measures that
generated a lot of pain on the streets of Greece (see LHS pic) The current status
about the Greece bailouts is that: (i) Greece exited its final official European bailout
program in August 2018 and continues to repay its remaining European loans ahead
of schedule; (ii) It has also fully repaying its roughly €28 billion in total debt owed to
the IMF ahead of schedule in April 2022 to lower borrowing costs.

What is the European Stability Mechanism (ESM)?

5

▪ ESM  is the Regional Financing Arrangement (RFA) with the biggest firepower in

the world. It is an international financial institution created in 2012 by the euro area
member states to provide financial assistance to member countries in financial
distress as an additional layer of Global Financial safety Net (GFSN),
complementing IMF lending. ESM has been coordinating with IMF on bailouts.

▪ The ESM evolved from the European Financial Stability Facility (EFSF) that was
set up as a temporary solution in June 2010 and granted assistance to three
countries – Ireland, Portugal & Greece. The EFSF still exists as a legal entity but
can no longer grant new loans.

▪ ESM, the successor to EFSF, was created with a subscribed capital of €704.8

billion (has only €80 bn in paid up capital) and a lending firepower capacity of €500
bn. It grants assistance only when it is deemed necessary to protect the financial
stability of the euro area and its member states and is provided under strict
conditionality. Total ledming by ESM/ESFS has been €295 bn so far

▪

It also operates as the euro area's crisis resolution mechanism,
offering loans and new capital to banks to safeguard the financial
stability of the entire euro area. As such, ESM also acts as a
backstop for the Single resolution Fund, which is designed as a
supranational emergency fund for resolution and restructuring of
failing banks that may have systemic implications.

▪ The ESM is headquartered in Luxembourg and is financed by
capital from its member states, making it a self-sustaining
institution that does not rely on EU taxpayer money for lending.

▪ Currently ESM has a total lending capacity of €500 billion.

Available Funds are approximately €422-433 billion that remains
available for new financial assistance. Outstanding loans are
around €74.2 billion in active loans are currently held by some
euro area member states, mainly some new debt to Greece
besides Spain, and Cyprus who also have borrowed. Spain is
projected to complete its final loan repayment by 2027

How did the EU and the ECB respond to the Euro area Crisis of 2019-12?

7

▪ ECB in Dec. 2011 introduced 3-year LTROs with a view to reduce obstacles to
credit supply through the mitigation of liquidity and funding risks. They were non-
standard monetary policy measures launched in Dec 2011 and March 2012 to
inject massive liquidity, stabilize interbank lending markets, and ease debt
pressures on peripheral EZ governments. They were done on fixed-rate tender
procedures tied to main policy rate with full allotment lasting 36 months, featuring
early repayment options after 1-year. one year.

▪

In June 2014, ECB started TLTROs under which banks borrowed from ECB
linked to their loans to non-financial corporations and households). Three rounds
announced on 5-Jun-14, 10-Mar-16 & 7-Mar-19. TLTRO-III was a series of 10
TLTROs, each with a maturity of three years, starting in Sept. 2019 at a quarterly
frequency. Borrowing rates in these operations were allowed to be as low as 50
basis points below the average interest rate on the deposit facility over the period
from 24 June 2020 to 23 June 2022 and, if applicable, as low as the average
interest rate on the deposit facility during the rest of the life of the same operation.
So they started at a favourable rate of -1% (when deposit facility rate was -0.5%)
and increasingly lower interest rates linked to larger bank lending was extended
into COVID-19 with 10 rounds conducted till Dec. 2021.

Draghi’s three words that changed the course of the Euro area

8

▪ On 26th July 2012, the President of the European Central

Bank (ECB), Mario Draghi, delivered a short speech
(remarks) at a Global Investment Conference in London that
brought the crucial turnaround in the euro crisis. He said,
“Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it
takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be
enough.”

▪ This was a unique forward guidance that changed the course
of the EZ crisis. Financial markets responded immediately.
Spreads on the government bonds of peripheral countries
dropped back from over 500 bps levels to a manageable
100-200 bps levels (see leftmost chart), This as pe some
estimates may have saved well over €200 bn in funding
costs to euro area countries. Equity markets also recovered
and euro recovered from the large earlier depreciation.
Business confidence improved and so helped bring an end
to recession in euro area  (see left side second chart).

▪ But the effects to real sector were not seamless and led to

increase in cash, leverage & lending to zombie firms

ECB’s OMT efforts in place of QE

9

▪ On 12th Aug 2012, the ECB Governing Council announced it will make outright secondary market transactions in sovereign
bonds aimed "at safeguarding an appropriate monetary policy transmission and the singleness of the monetary policy“. On
6th September 2012, the ECB announced the OMT framework with (with Bundesbank President, Jens Wideman sole
dissent vote). The framework for providing ECB buying 1-3 year govt bonds of EZ country) on four conditions:

➢ The country should have received macroeconomic financial support from the EZ’s bailout funds EFSF/ESM or

precautionary conditioned credit lines. (Spain having received it for bank recapitalization did not qualify).

➢ The country should have signed MOU on conditionalities (not under review for program compliance) attached to the

EFSF/ESM for sovereign support at the time of OMT purchases. The MOU stated country agreeing to certain domestic
economy measures (conditionality).

➢ OMT purchases can be started when the state has managed to regain complete access to private lending markets,

judged by successful issuance of a new government 10-year bond.

➢ OMT purchases will be done after ECB assesses above pre-conditions being met, finds that the market traded yields for

the government bonds are distressed (higher compared to what can be justified by the fundamental macro of the
country).

▪ OMT was to be fully sterilized. By the end-2014, only Portugal and Ireland qualified to receive OMT support and though
Greece & Cyprus were desperate for support, not having market access even in 2015. OMT was never actually used.

▪ OMT support was challenged in German Federal Constitutional Court by members of the German Bundestag but the

European Court of Justice ruled that it does not contravene the prohibition of monetary financing of EU nations

Lessons from the Euro Area Crisis

14

▪ Managing sovereign debt risks is important and AEs should not be lulled into complacency

that it is a risk only EMDEs face

▪ Avert diabolic loops of sovereign and bank debt

▪ Monetary Unions require certain conditions to work seamlessly.

▪

It is hard to keep monetary union without a fiscal union. Growth & Stability Pact in the EU
was weakly observed

▪ The crisis exposed the fragility of a monetary union without a unified banking

system. Divergences between Eurozone countries persisted due to a lack of common
supervision and resolution mechanisms

▪ Forward guidance can be a powerful tool in a crisis

▪ Legal frameworks for maintaining financial stability & crisis resolution should be clearly laid

down

▪ Regional Financial Arrangements (RFAs) may be needed to supplement other layers of

Global Financial Safety Net (GFSN)

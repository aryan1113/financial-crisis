What is not included in GDP
6
Specific exclusion from GDP includes the following:
 Intermediate Goods: Goods and services used to produce other goods or services
(e.g., the flour a baker buys to make bread) are excluded to avoid double-counting.
 Re-sales/ Used Goods/ Second hand purchases: The sale of items that were
produced in a previous period, such as used cars or homes, is not included in
current GDP.
 Non-Market Activities: Unpaid activities like household work (housewives doing
household chores, teaching kids, cooking food), volunteer work, and caregiving are
not counted because they are difficult to measure and value.
 Illegal or Underground Economy: While some authorities estimate these activities,
they are not formally included in GDP calculations due to their unrecorded nature.
 Transfer Payments: Payments made by the government for which no good or
service is received in return, such as social security or unemployment benefits, are
not included.
 Foreign-Owned Production: Profits earned by foreign companies within a country
and then sent back to their home country are not part of the domestic GDP.
 Unproductive or Wasteful Expenditures: The value of goods or services that do
not create wealth, such as the cost of preventing or responding to crime, are often
included in GDP, but they represent costs rather than production.

System of National Accounts (SNA)
10
SNA is the internationally agreed set of recommendations on how to compile measures of
economic activity.
 The First SNA was published in 1953 after a UN Sub-Committee report largely authored by
Richard Stone in 1947 laid a foundation for it.
 Since then, six revisions of SNA have been done (in 1960, 1964, 1968, 1993, 2008 & just now
in 2025). SNA 2025 has tried to integrate digital economy by capturing intangible assets, AI &
cloud computing, digital platforms, crypto assets & provides guidance on free digital services. It
has also included natural capital accounting updating the SEEA.
 SNA 2008 is a 1260-page technical document or a sort of manual for guidance in preparation of
national accounts by countries that like the 2008 version provides for a sequence of accounts
(including some volume accounting), balance sheets, supply and use tables and deals with
concepts, definitions, boundaries for production, consumption and assets. However, the 2025
SNA broadens the national accounts framework to better account for elements affecting well-
being and sustainability, which are essential for informing various policy goals like Islamic
finance, globalization, deglobalization
 Since the 1993 version, SNA is now being jointly produced by the UN, IMF, World Bank,
OECD and EU. It introduced SEEA satellite account.
 SNA helps in generation of internationally comparable GDP and other national accounting
metrics. It has links to monetary statistics, flow of funds, balance of payment accounts and also
has a set of satellite accounts.

What is Balance of Payments (BOP) &
Why do we need to compile & understand it?
12
 National economies today are not autarky. Therefore, we need financial
recording of one country dealing with another. International economics,
therefore, looks at the Balance of Payments account (also known
as balance of international payments and abbreviated BOP or BoP). It
records for a country the difference between all money flowing into the
country in a particular period of time (e.g., a quarter or a year) and the
outflow of money to the rest of the world.
 These financial transactions may be made by individuals, firms and
government bodies in the form of receipts and payments arising out of
trade of goods or services, or just current transfers by one entity to
another or in the form of cross-border financial asset flows.
 More precisely, the BoP systematically summarizes all economic
transactions in financial terms between the residents and the non-
residents of a country or of an economic area during a given period.

Usefulness of Balance of Payments (BOP)
18
 Usefulness of CAB - indicates whether economy is generating enough savings to finance
domestic investment. But it is not necessary that a current account deficit (CAD) is bad
and a CAB surplus is good. In fact, it makes sense for emerging market economies
(EMEs) to run CAD now and grow faster to achieve convergence in per capita incomes
with advanced economies (AEs) and run surpluses later to pay back debts. This
intergenerational transfers benefit the future generation as well.
 Deficit in BoP, if large and for long, raises sustainability issues - affecting growth,
employment and price stability. It requires corrective actions.
 BOP has great importance in the determination of monetary and fiscal policies. Deficits
have a strong impact on inflation through its effects on exchange rate and its expectations.
Depreciation may lead to expectations of further weakening and a downward spiral,
discouraging capital inflows, encouraging outflows, speculative attack on currency. One
can get imported inflation.
 Today, BoP statistics are not only used in determination of economic policy but are widely
employed by business enterprises e.g. tourism receipts are widely used by like hotel and
transport firms in their strategic planning. Trade statistics are used by commercial
enterprises to determine their size in the market for certain goods or ascertain potential
trade opportunities.

What are Current, Capital & Financial Account in BPM6?
21
 Capital Account: It covers all transactions that involve:
 (a) the receipt or payment of capital transfers (debt forgiveness, nonlife insurance claims, investment grants, one-
off guarantees and other debt assumption, capital taxes and other capital transfers) and
 (b) the acquisition/disposal of non-produced, non-financial assets, which includes transactions associated with
tangible assets (e.g., land and subsoil assets) and transactions associated with intangible assets (e.g., patents,
copyrights, trademarks, franchises, etc.)
 Financial Account: The Financial account covers all transactions associated with changes of ownership in the foreign
financial assets and liabilities of an economy. The financial account is broken down into five basic functional
categories:
 (a) Direct investment (divided by instrument into equity and investment fund shares, reinvestment of earnings and
debt instruments),
 (b) Portfolio investment (divided by instrument into equity and investment fund shares, reinvestment of earnings
for investment fund shares and debt securities),
 (c) Financial derivatives and employee stock options,
 (d) Other investment (divided by instrument into other equity, currency and deposits, loans, insurance, pension
schemes and other standardized guarantee schemes, trade credits and advances, other accounts
receivable/payable and special drawing rights) and
 (e) Official reserve assets (divided by instrument into monetary gold, special drawing rights, reserve position
in the International Monetary Fund and other reserve assets).

What is the difference between Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI) &
Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) flows?
 FPIs are entities which bring in investments in securities (equity/
debt/ other financial assets) in a country on behalf of investors in
another country. As per SEBI guidelines FPI can register as
Category I if they are from FATF jurisdictions and as Category II if they
are from non-FATF jurisdictions. FPI holdings can include stocks,
ADRs, GDRs, bonds, mutual funds, and exchange traded funds
(ETFs). FPI consists of passive ownership; investors have no control
or direct ownership of property or a stake in a company/ venture.
 FDI are equity flows that are classified as such if the investments
exceed 10% of paid-up-capital of a listed firm (OECD definition
adopted globally) and can have stake in the company/ venture.
All foreign equity investments in non-listed space are treated as
FDI. These are typically made by Private Equity (PE) funds in stocks
that are not publicly traded on exchanges. They typically use a range
of strategies such as leveraged buyouts (LBOs), venture capital (VCs),
growth, real estate private equity such as in REITS, investment private
equity such as in InvITs, mezannine capital, distressed PE,
secondaries and fund of funds.

What is Primary Income in the Current Account?
23
a.3 - Primary income (BOP item IN1)
Primary income covers three types of transactions between residents and non-residents.
 Compensation of employees records wages, salaries and other benefits, in cash or in kind, earned by
individuals for work performed for economic units whose place of residence is different from their own
(border workers, seasonal workers, employees of international organisations, etc.). BOP
item Compensation of employees records money paid to non-resident workers or received from non-
resident employers.
 Investment income is income derived from ownership of external financial assets and liabilities and payable
by residents of one economy to residents of another economy. In line with the related financial
account, Investment income is classified in Direct investment, Portfolio investment, and Other investment
and reserve assets income. It includes interest, dividends, remittances of branch profits, and direct
investor's shares of the retained earnings of direct investment enterprises; income on equity and
investment fund shares, as well as on debt securities from portfolio investment; income from interest,
withdrawals from income of quasi corporations and income attributable to policyholders in insurance,
pension schemes and standardized guarantee schemes.
 Other primary income includes rent, taxes and subsidies on products and production.

What is Secondary Income in the Current Account?
24
a.4 - Secondary income (BOP item IN2)
 It is a counterpart entry, required by the double-entry system used in BOP compilation, that offsets the provision of a
non-financial, or financial, item by a resident to a nonresident (or vice versa) without a counterpart return of an item of
economic value. Secondary income consists of all transfers, which are not capital (see below). Current transfers are
broken down, according to the sector of the compiling economy, into two subcomponents: general government and
other sectors.
 General government transfers are broken down into:
(a) Current taxes on income, wealth, etc.,
(b) Social contributions,
(c) Social benefits,
(d) Current international cooperation, and
(e) Miscellaneous current transfers.
 Other sectors' transfers are broken down into:
(a) Current taxes on income, wealth, etc.,
(b) Social contributions,
(c) Social benefits,
(d) Net nonlife insurance premiums,
(e) Nonlife insurance claims,
(f) Miscellaneous current transfers, which includes personal transfers between resident and non-resident
households, including workers' remittances.

Why does International Trade Take Place?
29
 Ricardian Theory: As countries differ in their technology to produce goods and services,
they specialize in the product they have comparative advantage in even if they do not have
an absolute advantage in that product. In the LHS example, Portugal has absolute
advantage in producing both cloth & wine but if labour is immobile, then both countries can
gain by producing as per the comparative advantage. England can use 220 hrs of labour to
produce 2.2 units of cloth (i.e 220/100) and Portugal can use 170 hrs of labour to produce
2.125 units of wine (i.e.170/80). Both countries are better off if England trades a unit of
cloth for in between 5/6 to 9/8 units of Portuguese wine.
 Heckscher-Ohlin Theory: As countries differ in their resource endowments skills and
abilities of a country’s workforce, the natural resources available within its borders
(minerals, farmland, etc.), and the sophistication of its capital stock (machinery,
infrastructure, communications systems), the basis of trade can be comparative advantage
in resource endowments. Countries in which capital is relatively plentiful and labour
relatively scarce will tend to export capital-intensive products and import labour-intensive
products, while countries in which labour is relatively plentiful and capital relatively scarce
will tend to export labour-intensive products and import capital-intensive products. It is
relative endowment (i.e. capital per worker) that matters.
 Strategic Trade Theory: Following Brander and Spencer, literature has proliferated using
microeconomic models and game theory to describe strategic interactions between firms in
an international oligopoly (small number of firms) matter for trade and how national policies
and government interventions play a key role affecting these strategic interaction giving
advantage to firms in trade.

While fiscal deficit deals with the government’s total borrowings including the interest payment,
primary deficit deals with the government’s borrowings excluding the interest payment.

How to Determine Debt Sustainability? … Domar Condition
47
 Any economic unit, entity and sector can take debt through some financial instrument which requires payment of interest
and principal at future date(s) as per contract. Failure to do amounts to debt default.
 When governments (national and sub-national) incurs debt, it is counted as public debt also called government liabilities.
The broadest definition of public debt can include obligations of state-owned enterprises but generally one focuses on
only government debt. Government also incurs contingent liabilities that may devolve on government if it is guaranteed
by it.
 The simplest thumb rule of sustainable public debt is that r-g<0; i.e. the growth rate of the economy exceeds the interest
rate. This was a rule given by Domar, Evsey D., “The Burden of Debt and National Income”, American Economic
Review, 3(4),798-827,1944
 In 2005, The IMF & the IDA arm of the World Bank introduced a Debt Sustainability Frameworks (DSF) for low-income
countries (LICs), which has been revised from time to time. The DSF uses a template for both external debt and
for public sector debt. Concessionality is an important element in this as a large part of the LIC debt is concessional and
focusing on the present value (PV) of debt requires that to be build into the Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA). Such
analysis throws debt-service dynamics under a baseline scenario and also provide standardized alternative scenarios
and stress tests. DSAs compares results with established thresholds and classifies it under four categories: low risk
(when there are no breaches of thresholds); moderate risk (when thresholds are breached in risk scenarios);
high risk (when thresholds are breached in the baseline scenario); and in debt distress (when a distress event,
like arrears or a restructuring, has occurred or is considered imminent).
 LICs with unsustainable public debt typically seek IMF financial assistance and/or seek support from Paris Club, set up
in 1956, which is a group of officials from major creditor countries whose role is to find coordinated and sustainable
solutions to the payment difficulties experienced by debtor countries. As debtor countries undertake reforms to stabilize
and restore their macroeconomic and financial situation, Paris Club creditors provide an appropriate debt treatment.
Paris Club creditors provide debt relief by concessional rescheduling or reduction in debt service obligations during a
defined period (flow treatment) or as of a set date (stock treatment). A similar treatment is negotiated for private creditors
under the London Club set up in 1976.

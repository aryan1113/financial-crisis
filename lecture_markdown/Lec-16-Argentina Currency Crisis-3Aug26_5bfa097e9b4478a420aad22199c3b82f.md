Argentina: A History of Financial Crises & Sovereign Debt Defaults … (1):
The 1975 Rodrigazo Plan

2

▪

In 1973, Argentine economy was tottering under President Cámpora whose Minister for
Economy, Jóse Ber Gelbard, a Polish immigrant businessman, had close ties with
Communist party. He had founded Confederación General Económica (General Economic
Confederation or the CGE). He was a trusted associate of Peronists. Gelbard implemented a
Keynesian Plan to increase real wages in order to strengthen the aggregate demand but then
followed it up with heterodox shock of wage and price freeze as the OPEC oil price shock
derailed any chance of its success.

▪ Gelbard hoped to save his plan by signing trade agreements with socialist countries in
Eastern Europe and China, but in this era of Cold War, the U.S. moved in to thwart its
efforts by countermeasures such as the blockade of the sale to Cuba of cars manufactured by
U.S. companies in Argentina.

▪ 1975 Rodrigazo: Rodrigazo is the name given to a group of economic policies announced in

Argentina on June 4, 1975. They were credited to the Minister of Economy, Celestino
Rodrigo, soon after his appointment in May by President Isabel Perón. It included:
➢ a 150% devaluation of currency,
➢ a 180% increase in fuel price,
➢ a 100% increase in utility & transportation prices &
➢ a 45% increase in wages. Riots followed. The President was thrown off in a coup on 24-

Mar-1976.

▪ Argentina extended $1.2 bn loan to Cuba on 28-Aug-73 that was never repaid & drained its

FX reserves.

Argentina: A History of Financial Crises & Sovereign Debt Defaults … (2):
The 1989 Hyperinflation & the Failed Primavera Plan

3

▪ The 1989 Hyperinflation: It was a catastrophic economic crisis characterized by annual inflation
rates exceeding 3,000%. Banking system collapsed after decades of chronic budget shortfalls that
forced the government to rely heavily on inflationary money printing. The August 1988
"Primavera Plan" miserably failed. drove the central bank into severe debt distress. The plan was
based on a heterodox economic stabilization program and included (i) Price  agreements with the
Argentine Industrial Union (UIA) and the Chamber of Commerce to implement a system of
decreasing, controlled monthly price increases. In exchange, the Value Added Tax (VAT) was cut
from 18% to 15%, (ii) Split Exchange Market where in the government instituted a multi-tier
exchange rate system. Agricultural exports were liquidated at a lower commercial rate, while
industrial exports and financial transactions were handled via a higher, semi-free financial rate,
(iii) State Austerity by freezing public sector hiring, suspending salary negotiations with state
unions, and  plans for quick privatization of state-owned companies and (iv) Price hikes by
increasing rates for public utilities by 30% right before freezing costs.
Inflation temporarily dropped to single digits by Dec 1988 but the plan failed due to severe
shortage of FX reserves. With food riots, political uncertainty mounted. High domestic debt burden
and interest obligations eroded public sector surpluses, triggering a complete loss of market
confidence. In February 1989, World Bank suspended its financial aid to Argentina. This triggered
a massive run on the Argentine currency (the austral). The Central Bank sold roughly $900 million
of reserves in just a few days trying to defend the currency before abandoning the peg. As inflation
returned, local currency savings became completely worthless, prompting people to hoard US
dollars. Resultant dollarization further eroded monetary control. President Raúl Alfonsín had to
resign and hand over power to President Carlos Menem early in July 1989 amidst the chaos.

▪ On April 1, 1991, Argentina established its currency board as part of its Convertibility Plan, by
pegging the Argentine peso 1:1 with the USD but this prevented the government from devaluing
the currency to restore market balance. The Plan was shaped by President Carlos Menem with
his Economy Minister Domingo Cavallo & required (i) the central bank to back at least two-
thirds (and eventually all) of the domestic monetary base with international U.S. dollar reserves
and (ii) banned indexation and prohibited the central bank from printing money to finance
government budget deficits.

▪ Argentine Crash (1998–2002): As a fallout of East Asian financial crisis (1997-98), followed by
the Russian GKO crisis (Aug. 1998) & the Brazilian currency crisis (Aug-Oct 1998), financial
crisis spilled over to Argentina after a deep recession hit the country in Q3: 2008 and lasted till
Q2: 2002. It came to be called the 1998–2002 Argentine Great Depression. The depression
culminated in Dec 2001 with the largest sovereign debt default in history at the time ($95 billion),
freezing of bank accounts (corralito), and a political crisis resulting in five presidents in two
weeks. The corralito was a severe economic restriction in Argentina that limited bank cash
withdrawals to $250 per week. It was imposed by Economy Minister Domingo Cavallo under
President Fernando de la Rúa on Dec 1, 2001. It caused mass protests, and triggered a massive
political crisis.

▪ Over 50% of Argentines fell below the poverty line & 25% became indigent by 2002. Political

upheaval with widespread riots and protests followed in Dec. 2001 & led to multiple presidential
resignations. Argentina declared one of the largest sovereign debt defaults in history and
abandoned the fixed peso-dollar peg.

Argentina: A History of Financial Crises & Sovereign Debt Defaults … (4):
The 2014 Technical Sovereign Default

5

Argentina Bond Spreads (EMBI+)

▪ It was a selective sovereign default resulting from prolonged legal battles with holdout

hedge funds ("vulture funds") over restructured debt. On July 30, 2014, Argentina failed
to pay $539 million in interest to exchange bondholders because a U.S. court blocked the
funds. This crisis stemmed from a long legal battle with U.S. hedge funds ("holdouts")
that refused past debt restructuring deals.

▪ Holdout creditors were ~ 7% of bondholders who rejected Argentina's 2005 and 2010

debt restructuring swaps (which offered roughly 30 cents on the dollar) and instead sued
it for 100% face value. U.S. District Judge Thomas Griesa ruled that Argentina could
not pay the 93% of compliant exchange bondholders unless it simultaneously paid the
holdout hedge funds in full under pari passu (equal treatment) terms.

▪ Argentina invoked RUFO Clause Constraint arguing it could not pay the holdout

funds without triggering the RUFO (Rights Upon Future Offers) clause in its
restructured contracts, which would have legally allowed compliant bondholders to
demand equal higher payouts—creating an unaffordable $100 billion liability. S&P
downgraded Argentina's rating to "selective default". Argentina resolved the 2014
technical default in February 2016 after reaching a $4.6 billion agreement with holdout
hedge funds.

 IMF response to the series of Currency Crises during 2013-2025

7
▪ 2018 Stand-By Arrangement (SBA):

➢ Approval Date: June 20, 2018 (under President Mauricio Macri)
➢ Initial Amount: US$50 billion, later augmented to about US$56.3 billion
➢ Core Goal: Restore market confidence, reduce public financing needs, and place debt on a downward path following sharp

currency depreciation.

➢ Outcome: The program went off-track amid deep economic contraction and political transition.

▪ 2022 Extended Fund Facility (EFF):

➢ Approval Date: March 25, 2022 (under President Alberto Fernández)
➢ Amount: US$44 billion (SDR 31.914 billion) designed to replace the 2018 SBA.
➢ Core Goal: Strengthen public finances, combat persistent high inflation, protect social spending, and refinance prior debt.
➢ Outcome: Suffered continuous target slippages due to severe droughts and macro imbalances.

▪ 2025–2026 Extended Fund Facility (EFF):

➢ Approval Date: April 11, 2025 (48-month arrangement under President Javier Milei)
➢ Amount: ~US$21 billion (SDR 15.267 billion) = 479% of Quota; the second review was completed on May 21, 2026, unlocking

an additional ~US$1 billion.

➢ Core Goal: Entrench disinflation, rebuild foreign exchange reserves, phase out capital controls, and support sustainable private-

sector growth.

➢ Current Status: Active in 2026, supported by structural fiscal consolidation and recent legislative reforms.

 IMF Conditionalities for Argentina’s 2025-26 EFF

8
▪ Quantitative Performance Criteria (QPCs)

➢ Primary Fiscal Balance: Cumulative floors on the federal government primary surplus, targeting roughly 1.4% to 2.5%

of GDP to maintain deep spending discipline.

➢ International Reserves: Periodic quantitative floors set for net international reserves accumulation by the central bank
(BCRA), adjusted to handle external shocks. NIR accumulation targets for mid-2025 and end-2025 were missed due to
capital outflows and dollarization, requiring waivers of non-observance and target modifications.

➢ Monetary Financing: Strict ceilings prohibiting direct central bank financing of the government deficit.

➢ Domestic Arrears: Ceilings limiting the federal government stock of domestic payment arrears.

▪ Policy Conditions and Structural Reforms

➢ Fiscal Consolidation: Enforcing strict expenditure controls while preserving spending space for targeted universal child

and food assistance social programs.

➢ Monetary and FX Framework: Refining monetary operations to lower interest rate volatility, ease capital account/FX

restrictions, and improve policy transmission.

➢ Debt Management: Implementing a strategy to extend peso debt maturities and substitute inflation- or FX-linked

securities with fixed-rate instruments.

➢ Structural Benchmarks: Advancing key structural legislations covering fiscal efficiency, trade, and labour reform

streams.

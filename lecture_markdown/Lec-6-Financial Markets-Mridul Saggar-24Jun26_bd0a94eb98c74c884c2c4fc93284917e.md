Lombard Street and the Money Markets in History
2
▪ Walter Bagehot (1826-1877) was one of the first writers to describe and explain
the money markets to the world of banking and finance in this book which is a
treatise. The book was initially printed in Great Britain by Henry S. King & Co. in
1873.
▪ Bagehot in his book argued that money markets were something real and have
got developed as a sophisticated loan-fund in Lombard Street, acting as a crucial
intermediary between savings in agricultural areas and the industrial demands of
growing urban centers, promoting a functioning economy reliant on borrowed
capital.
▪ The book was written as a reaction to the financial collapse of Overend, Gurney
& Co., a wholesale discount bank located at 65 Lombard Street - a historic
center of the London banking industry from medieval times to the 1980s. When
Gurney & Co suspended payments on 10 May 1866, panic spread across
London, Liverpool, Manchester, Norwich, Derby and Bristol
▪ BAGEHOT RULE: To avert a financial crisis driven by panic, the central bank
should lend early and liberally to solvent firms facing liquidity problems against
good collateral at high rates

What is Money Market?
3
▪ Money Market is today clearly defined as a marketplace where banks or other
financial intermediaries exchange short-term funds or financial instruments for a
period of a year or less.
▪ In India, the concept of money market funds can be traced to pre-Independence
era but the market was largely unorganized and segmented in those days
▪ In 1985 when the money market settlement fund was established to diversify
stock investments and enhance liquidity in the financial system.
▪ In 1987, the RBI Report of the Working Group on the Money Market (Chairman:
N. Vaghul) laid down a roadmap for development of the money market in India
and made recommendations for improving functioning of call money market,
developing bills rediscounting market, introducing short-term money market
operations in government paper, developing other money market instruments like
inter-bank participation certificates & CDs and setting up a finance house to
discount papers.
▪ Since that point money markets in India have evolved, grown in size and become
important part of the integrated financial markets here volatilities spillover.

Money Markets in India…(2): The Repo Market
5
▪ Repurchase Agreements (Repos): A repo is a contractual arrangement between two parties,
where one party agrees to sell securities to another party at a specified price with a commitment to
buy the securities back at a later date for another (usually higher) specified price. A mirror image of
this transaction will be the Reverse Repo, where one party agrees to buy the security from another
counterparty with a commitment to reverse it after a day or a pre-agreed time period. If the
transactions are done without a commitment to reverse the deal, they become outright transactions
and they do not fall under repo markets.
▪ Repos are conducted by central bank with commercial banks (central bank repo) or by two eligible
counterparties directly (market repo). Such transactions for tenors up to 1-year is classified as a
money market instrument. Longer-term repos that are not part of money market can also be done.
However, the overnight segment is dominant in the repo market.
▪ At present, repoable securities include Central Government dated securities (G-Secs), Treasury Bills
(T-Bills), State Development Loans (SDLs) and Corporate Bonds. Recently, RBI also permitted re-
repo of securities acquired under reverse repo subject to certain restrictions. Such an arrangement
is permitted only in case of dated G-Secs, T-Bills and SDLs.

Money Markets in India…(3): The CROMS in the Repo Market
6
▪ The entities permitted to undertake repo transactions include Scheduled Commercial Banks,
Co-operative Banks, Primary Dealers, Mutual Funds, Insurance Companies and corporate
entities. These authorized entities use CROMS to conduct both Basket Repos (where a
member can borrow against any one of the security forming part of the basket) and Special
Repos (where a member borrows against specific Central or State Government security rather
than a general pool of securities) and to execute Government Securities Lending Transactions
(GSLT) where an entity which is Gilt Account Holders (GAH) temporarily borrows one specific
government security against another security for a fee.
▪ Clearcorp Repo Order Matching System (CROMS) is an STP enabled anonymous Order
Matching Platform launched by Clearcorp Dealing Systems (India) Ltd, a wholly-owned
subsidiary of CCIL, for facilitating dealing in Market Repos in all kinds of Government
Securities. OTC transactions (where counterparties are known) too are permitted on this
platform & reported. However, market has migrated substantially to Order Matching & OTC
trades on Clearcorp or old style outside Clearcorp are now few.

Money Markets in India…(4): Tripartite Repos (TREPS)
7
▪ Collateralized Borrowing & Lending Obligation (CBLO): CBLO was a money market instrument
introduced with effect from January 20, 2003 by the Clearing Corporation of India (CCIL). CCIL was
set up with support of the RBI in April 2001 and since 2014 has been acting as a as a Qualified
Central Counterparty (QCCP) for RBI regulated markets. A CCP is a financial institution that takes
on counterparty credit risk between parties to a transaction. CCIL provides clearing and settlement
services for trades in money, G-sec & FX markets.
▪ TREPS (Tri-party repo): is a repo contract where a third entity (apart from the borrower and lender),
called a Tri-Party Agent, acts as an intermediary between the two parties to the repo to facilitate
services like collateral selection, payment and settlement, custody and management during the life of
the transaction. CCIL introduced TREPS from November 3, 2018 under RBI guidelines replacing
CBLO. TREPS is very much like CBLO with anonymity of the counterparties assured in both cases
but in TREPS. However, TREPS amounted to legal recognition of CCIL as a third repo agent.
▪ Eligible entities for TREPS transactions are Scheduled Commercial banks (SCBs) including
Payment Banks & Small Finance Banks, Financial Institutions, Insurance Companies, Mutual Funds
(MFs), Primary Dealers (PDs) – standalone or bank PDs, NBFCs, Corporates including SEs & their
clearing corps, Provident/ Pension Funds, are participants. These are wide set of repo eligible
entities. Retail/ individual investors are prohibited from direct participation.
▪ CCIL operates the F-TRAC platform, which is the designated trade repository for reporting
secondary market trades in corporate bonds, CPs & CDs.

Money Markets in India… (4): Other Instruments including
T-bills, CPs, CDs, ABCPs
8
▪ Commercial Papers (CPs): They are unsecured, short-term debt instrument issued by non-
financial corporates (firms), NBFCs, AIFIs and some other permitted entities. Issuing entities must
have a minimum credit rating of P2 from CRISIL or equivalent rating. They typically finance short-
term liabilities such as payroll, accounts payable and inventories. CPs are usually issued at a
discount from face value with discount rate reflecting prevailing market interest rates. They have a
maturity from 7-days to 1-year.
▪ Certificate of Deposits (CDs): It is an unsecured money market instrument issued by a bank as a
usance promissory note against funds deposited with it for a maturity period up to one year. It is in
nature of saving deposit.
▪ Treasury Bills (T-bills): They are issued as promissory notes of up to 1-year by GoI. GOI issues
T-bills of tenors 91-day, 182-day & 364-days. They are zero coupon securities issued at a discount
to the face value.
▪ Asset Backed Commercial Papers (ABCPs): It is a short-term secured money market instrument
issued by corporates, banks, and financial institutions. Unlike normal commercial paper, it is
secured by assets such as trade receivables funded by issuance of securities. Banks use ABCP as
conduits to put their current asset credits off their balance sheets and yet provide liquidity support
to their clients. Currently, ABCPs are NOT PERMITTED in INDIA.

Money Markets in India… (5):
Money Market Mutual Funds (MMMFs)
9
▪ Money Market Mutual Funds (MMMF): They are open-ended mutual funds which in short-term, low-risk money
market (debt) instruments such as T-bills, CPs, CDs with an aim to benefit from lower volatility of short-term
instruments so as to maintain stable net asset value (NAV).
▪ MMMFs originated in the U.S. in 1972 as largely to workaround the Regulation Q that limited the interest rates
depository institutions were allowed to pay depositors. As interest rates rose in the 1970s, Regulation Q gave
depositors an incentive to find short-term investments outside of the banking system, such as T-bills, CPs &
CDs. MMMFs offered consumers the ability to invest in these instruments. The number of MMMFs in the U.S.
grew rapidly to 36 in 1975, 90 in 1980, 649 in 1990, Thereafter, on the back of money market defaults (in CPs in
1989 & 1990 and in derivatives in 1994), consolidation (M&A) and strict regulatory reforms followed (SEC’s Rule
2a-7 mandated stricter liquidity and credit quality limitation; & post GFC floating NAV which requirement by
SEC). Consequently, the number of MMFS has dropped to prompted many MMMFs to shift from prime funds to
govt only funds) the number of MMFs in the US has dropped to about 250 current;y. However, their total assets
has continued to grow and total over US$8 tn today.
▪ In India. MMMFs were started in 1992 & they were brought under SEBI regulation in 2000.
▪ Since MMMFs are not banks, they have grown outside the sphere of central banks regulation and supervision
and generally carry greater default risks than a bank or even an NBFC. In case interest rates rise abruptly they
can book losses on their portfolio of money market securities (CPs, CDs whose price falls like that of fixed
income securities like bonds). However, their investments in overnight or very short-term instruments offer offset.

Money Markets often give the first sign of trouble brewing in the
markets; act as leading indicator of institutional or systemic failures
11
▪ Money markets act as the plumbing of the global financial system. These markets show signs of strain, often
as a leading indicator of systemic financial stress - signalling through liquidity hoarding, credit tightening, or
volume freezes indicating lack of trust between financial institutions:
▪ Elevated Short-Term Rates: Unusual spikes in overnight rates (e.g., SOFR, SONIA, ESTR) above a central
bank's target rate (like the WACR or Interest on Reserve Balances-IORB) can point to an unexpected
shortage of cash and heightened competition for funding
▪ Widening Interest Rate Spreads: The difference between risk-free reference rate that are often risky
unsecured short-term lending rates (such as the earlier benchmarks LIBOR/Euribor or their new
replacements such as SOFR, SONIA, ESTR) and the sovereign risk-free rates [such as G-sec benchmarks,
overnight indexed swaps (OIS) or Treasury bills (T-bills) rates] measures the perceived risk of bank defaults.
Widening spreads show that banks are charging a premium to lend to one another, signalling credit fears.
▪ Shift to Safe Assets: During stressed periods, demand surges for ultra-safe instruments like T-bills.
Investors may also pull funds out of private money market funds if they fear defaults.
▪ Central Bank Facility Usage: A sudden spike in reliance on standing repo facilities indicates or discount
windows indicate that financial institutions are struggling to secure necessary funding from private
counterparties.

Bond Basics… (1): What is a Bond?
13
▪ Bonds are fixed income instruments that are issued and traded in debt capital markets as securities
with maturities longer than one year. This differentiates them from money market securities.
▪ Bonds have more intricate cash flow patterns than money market securities, which usually have just
one cash flow at maturity. This makes bonds more involved to price than money market instruments,
and their prices more responsive to changes in the general level of interest rates.
▪ There are a large variety of bonds. The most common type is the plain vanilla (or straight,
conventional or bullet) bond. This is a bond paying a regular (annual or semi–annual) fixed interest
payment or coupon over a fixed period to maturity or redemption, with the return of principal (the par
or nominal value of the bond) on the maturity date. All other bonds are variations on this.
▪ A Bond is a contractual agreement between the issuer and the bondholders. There are three elements
that an investor needs to know about when investing in a bond:
➢ The bond’s features, including issuer, maturity, par value, and coupon rate and frequency, and
currency denomination. These features determine the bond’s cash flows and therefore are key
determinants of investor’s expected and actual return
➢ The legal, regulatory and tax considerations that apply to the contractual agreement between the
issuer and bondholders
➢ The contingency provisions the may affect the bond’s scheduled cash flows. These contingency
provisions are options; they give the issuer or bondholders certain rights affecting the bond’s
disposal or redemption.

Bond Basics… (2): Some basic concepts related to Bonds?
14
▪ Maturity date of a bond refers to the date when the issuer is obligated to redeem the bond by paying the
outstanding principal amount. The tenor is the time remaining until bond’s maturity date
▪ What is a Bond’s Par Value? The principal of a bond is the amount the issuer agrees to repay the bondholders
on the maturity date. This amount is also called the par value, face value, nominal value, redemption
value or maturity value
▪ What is a Bond’s Coupon? The coupon rate of a bond is the interest rate that the issuer agrees to pay each
year until the maturity date. The annual amount of interest payments made is called the coupon. Coupon
payments can be made annually, semi-annually, quarterly or monthly.
▪ A plain vanilla bond pays a fixed rate of interest. The coupon payment does not change during the life of the
bond. A Floating Rate Notes (FRNs) or Floaters pay a floating rate of interest. The coupon rate of a FRN
includes two components – a reference rate plus a spread. The spread is typically constant and expressed
in bips(basis points). A bip (basis point) is equal to 0.01%. For example, an Indian FRN may have the rate
specified as MIBOR/ SORR + 0.50%, which means that the coupon rate for this bond would be
prevailing Mumbai Inter Bank Offer Rate/ Secured Overnight Financing Rate plus 0.50%. MIBOR is a collective
name for a set of rates covering different lending offers among banks for different maturities ranging from
overnight to one year. As part of the international benchmark reforms, RBI since Dec 2024 MIBOR with SORR
and this alignment is underway. A Zero-Coupon Bond do not pay interest, instead they are issued at a discount
to par value and redeemed at par value.

Bond Basics… (3): Yield to Maturity (YTM)
15
▪ The ‘current yield’ of a bond is equal to bond’s annual coupon divided by the bond’s price, expressed
as a percentage.
▪ The ‘Yield to Maturity (YTM)’ or ’Yield to Redemption’ or ’Yield to call’ is the internal rate of return on a
bond’s expected cash flows – that is the discount rate that equates the present value of bond’s
expected cash flows until maturity with the bond’s price.
▪ There is an inverse relationship between a bond’s price and its YTM, all else being equal. That is, the
higher the bonds YTM, the lower its price. Thus, investors anticipating a lower interest rate environment
(in which investors demand lower YTM) hope to earn a positive return from price appreciation
▪ Duration of a bond is a way to measure how sensitive bonds are to changes in interest rates. It is
expressed as a number of years because it’s a calculation of how long it will take the investor to be paid
back on their loan in full. The further away a bond is from its maturity date, the longer its duration usually
is – meaning it is more exposed to changing interest rates.

Usually, the longer a bond is from maturity, the greater interest rate risk it

Bond Basics… (5): Duration of a Bond
17
▪ Duration of a bond is a way to measure how sensitive bonds are
to changes in interest rates. It is expressed as a number of years
because it’s a calculation of how long it will take the investor to be
paid back on their loan in full. The further away a bond is from its
maturity date, the longer its duration usually is – meaning it is more
exposed to changing interest rates. So, by changing average
maturity of one’s bond portfolio, one may be able to effect changes
in duration.
▪ A higher duration means a bond price is more volatile in response
to interest rate changes.
▪ Duration is affected by a bond’s maturity, coupon payments, and
yield, with longer maturities and lower coupon payments generally
resulting in higher durations.
▪ Duration is important for portfolio management as the treasuries
and CFOs seek to immunize themselves against interest rate risk or
to optimize returns amid risk-return trade offs.

Bond Basics… (6): Convexity of a Bond
18
▪ Bond Convexity measures the non-linear relationship
between bond’s price and its yield. It indicates how
much a bond’s duration changes as interest rates
change, with high convexity indicating price gains from
falling interest rates are greater than price losses from
rising interest rates of same magnitude.
▪ Most bond portfolios are dominated by bonds with
positive convexity, implying investors stand to benefit
with falling interest rate environments.
▪ Duration assumes a linear relationship between a
bond’s price and interest rate changes, but this is an
approximation. Convexity accounts for actual curved,
or convex relationships.
▪ Positive convexity means that as interest rates fall,
bond duration increases. This accelerates the bond
price increase.

Bond Basics… (7): Coupon Payment Structures of Bonds
19
▪ Fixed Rate structure promises a fixed rate of coupon paid on due date
▪ Floating Rate structure will not have a fixed coupon, instead their coupon rate is linked to an external reference
rate, such as MIBOR
▪ Step-up Rate structures may be fixed or floating, but increases by specified margins at specified dates.
▪ Credit-linked Rate structure has a coupon that changes when the bond’s credit rating changes
▪ Payment-in-Kind structure allows the issuer to pay interest in the form of additional amounts of the bond issue
rather than as a cash payment
▪ Deferred Coupon Rate structure or Split Coupon Rate structure pays no coupons for its first few years but then
pays a higher coupon than it otherwise normally would for the remainder of its life.
▪ Bullet Bond is a plain vanilla bond with one coupon payment.
▪ Amortizing Bond makes periodic payments of interest and repayments of principal. It is a fully
amortized bond if these fixed periodic payments reduces the bond’s outstanding principal amount to zero by the
maturity date. If not, it is a partially amortized bond that will have a balloon (large) payment at maturity to
retire bond’s outstanding principal amount.
▪ Perpetual Bond will have coupon payments perpetually with no retirement date.

Ratings of the Bonds: What are its Key Components
23
▪ Credit risk is the risk of loss that a lender may face due to the borrower’s failure to repay debt.
▪ There are two key components of credit risk:
– Whether the debt is likely to be paid on time? ➔ Usually referred to as Probability of Default (PD)
– In case of a default, how much loss will the lender incur? ➔ Usually referred to as Loss Given Default
(LGD)
▪ Conventional credit ratings assess the PD of the issuer.
▪ For credit risk assessment, it is important to have clarity on the definition of default.
– CRAs: Typically follow a default definition of delay of even one day of even one rupee.
– Banks and FIs: Typically follow a definition of delay beyond 90 days (tied to NPA recognition).
▪ In the rest of the presentation, we will cover the conventional credit ratings that address PD.

Green Bonds

▪ Green bonds refer to debt securities issued to raise capital earmarked for green projects. The exact
definition, however, varies depending on what constitutes green projects. For example, the Green Bond
Principles (GBPs), which were established in 2014 and are maintained by the International Capital Market
Association (ICMA), provide guidelines and green project categories (ICMA 2021). The Climate Bonds
Standards (CBSs), built on top of the GBPs by the Climate Bonds Initiative (CBI), provide a sector-specific
definition of “green” and are used for the certification of green bonds by CBI (Climate Bonds Initiative 2019)
▪ The origin of the Green bonds can be traced to the bond issued by the European Investment Bank in 2007
as the first green bond. The Green Bond Principles introduced by the International Capital Market
Association (ICMA) in 2014 provided a set of voluntary principles that promote transparent and
standardized climate objectives and impacts that qualify for a green label. Increasingly originated by
financial institutions, sovereigns, and nonfinancial corporates, green bond issuances have grown
rapidly over the last decade, reaching more than US$600 billion in 2021. Green bonds are one of the
most important types of climate-related bonds, representing one-half of the global total issuances of
climate-related bonds and about 80 percent of climate-related bond issuances in EMDEs
▪ Several sovereigns has issued green bonds since 2016.

the disclosure requirement emanating from the NVF+G of MCA.
▪ In December 2018, the National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct, 2018 (NGRBC), an
improvement over the NVGs, were issued. In March 2015, The International Capital Market Association
(ICMA) published a document called Green Bond Principles (GBP). The GBPs were a set of best practices
for issuing green bonds.
▪ SEBI thought fit to fill the vacuum caused by lack of regulation which resulted in many Indian players
tapping the overseas market for green / sustainable finance. SEBI initiated a consultation process for
disclosure requirements for Public Issue and Listing of Green Bonds and listing of privately placed Green
Bonds. A concept paper based upon the Green Bond Principles, 2015 of the ICMA was placed on the SEBI
website on December 3, 2015, for seeking public comments. After taking public comments, SEBI issued its
First Green Bonds Framework in May 2017. gaining from the experience it issued a New Green Bond
Framework applicable for all green bond issuances since April 1, 2023

Financing Pattern of the Firms
33
▪ In your Finances courses you would have already studied the issues relating to capital structure - the debt
vs equity choices a firms has to make
▪ Modigliani-Miller (MM) proposition-I says that the value of an unlevered firm is the same as the value of the
levered firm. So, the capital structure does not matter. It was based on the assumption that companies are
operating in the world of perfectly efficient markets, they do have not pay any taxes, the trading of securities
is executed without any transaction costs, bankruptcy takes place without any bankruptcy costs, and perfect
information symmetry prevails.
▪ Modigliani-Miller (MM) proposition-II states that the company’s cost of equity is directly proportional to the
company’s leverage level. An increase in leverage level induces a higher default probability for a company.
Therefore, investors tend to demand a higher cost of equity (return) to be compensated for the additional
risk. However, tax shields may make the cost of equity less sensitive to the leverage level.
▪ Information asymmetry theory states that firms prefer internal finance (own funds, viz, equity) to external
finance (borrowed funds viz bonds/ debentures, and long-term debt). The other way to look at this is that
firms will choose retained profits (internal equity), raising fresh equity (external equity) and debt
(borrowings) in decreasing order of preference for raising finance as controlling shareholders and
management want to avoid scrutiny that comes with raising fresh equity and even more so in the case of
bank borrowings.

Financing of the Corporates: The Pecking Order theory of Capital Structure
34
▪ How do Corporates finance themselves? What are firms’ sources (financing pattern) and uses of finance
(investment & other spending)?
▪ In his Presidential Address to the American Finance Association Stewart Myres pointed out that the capital
structure puzzle is far more complex that even the dividend puzzle. John Lintner's model of 1956 explains how
firms set dividends despite the MM irrelevance but we do not know how firms choose the debt, equity or hybrid
securities they issue & advanced the pecking order theory propounded in a seminal paper by Myers & Majluf
(1984)
▪ Myers and Majluf (1984) pointed to the information asymmetry that exists in financial markets stating that
management knows more about the true value of the firm than the investors and, therefore, they rely more on
internal sources of finance (retained earnings) and prefer debt to equity if external finance is to be used.
▪ Fazzari, et al (1984) extended the argument of information asymmetry in capital markets and explained that a
firm's internal cash flow may affect investment spending because of a "financing hierarchy" in which internal
funds have a cost advantage over new debt or equity finance. Financial constraints in capital markets can
magnify the macroeconomic effect of shocks to cash flow or liquidity that reduce some firms' access to low-
cost finance and worsen their balance sheet positions.
▪ Ajit Singh (1995), showed that financing pattern of the firms in developing economies differ from those in
developed countries as they rely more on external finance and more on external equity than debt finance.

What is the Government Securities (G-sec) Market?
38
▪ G-sec market is where debt instruments issued by the Government (both central and states)
are traded for meeting its financing requirements. The first issuances of the G-sec are made in
primary market, while subsequent buying and selling of the issued security is traded in the
secondary market.
▪ The challenge before the G-sec market functioning is t ensure that the increased borrowing
requirements of the Government, to finance high fiscal deficits is met in a cost-effective manner
without distorting the financial system (by repressed yields rather than market clearing yields).
▪ The supply of government securities is generally exogenous to the market, determined mainly
by the fiscal policy of the Government.
▪ From the perspective of an issuer, i.e., the Government, a deep and liquid government
securities market facilitates its borrowings from the market at reasonable cost.
▪ As stipulated under the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003, the
Reserve Bank has withdrawn from participating in the primary market for government securities
from April 1, 2006.

Institutional Structure of G-sec market in India
45
▪ As the debt manager to the central and state governments, the Reserve Bank manages the government
market borrowing programme in a non-disruptive manner while adhering to the broad objectives of cost
optimisation, risk mitigation and market development.
▪ The Internal Debt Management Department (IDMD) of the Reserve Bank is entrusted with the
responsibility of managing the domestic debt of the central government by statute vide Sections 20 and
21 of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Act, 1934, and of 28 state governments and two union territories
(UTs) in accordance with respective bilateral agreements as provided in Section 21A of the RBI Act.
▪ Public Debt Management Cell, Budget Division, Department of Economic Affairs, Ministry of Finance,
Govt. of India- Plan borrowings of the GoI, including market borrowings, other domestic borrowings,
SGBs, manage Central Government’s liabilities including NSSF, contingent liabilities.
▪ Each state government is responsible for their own debt servicing and redemption.
▪ The Public Debt Office (PDO) of the Reserve Bank of India acts as the registry / depository of G-Secs and
deals with the issue, interest payment and repayment of principal at maturity.
▪ Clearing Corporation of India Ltd. (CCIL) - For secondary market operations (also through stock

Operational Aspects of the Issuance of G-Sec … (1)
46
▪ Main Participants in the auction process- Commercial banks, scheduled UCBs, primary
dealers (PDs), insurance companies and provident funds, who maintain funds account
(current account) and securities accounts (Subsidiary General Ledger (SGL) account)
with RBI.
▪ The results of the auction are published by RBI at stipulated time.
▪ All non-e-Kuber members including non-scheduled UCBs can participate in the primary
auction through scheduled commercial banks or PDs (called as Primary Members-
PMs).
▪ For this purpose, the UCBs/individuals etc. need to open a securities account with a
bank / PD – such an account is called a Gilt Account. A Gilt Account is a dematerialized
account maintained with a scheduled commercial bank or PD. The proprietary
transactions in G-Secs undertaken by PMs are settled through SGL account maintained
by them with RBI at Public Debt Office (PDO).
▪ The transactions in G-Secs undertaken by Gilt Account Holders (GAHs) through their
PMs are settled through Constituent Subsidiary General Ledger (CSGL) account
maintained by PMs with RBI at PDO for its constituent (e.g., a non-scheduled UCB).

Operational Aspects of the Issuance of G-Sec … (2)
47

▪ Settlement for the securities auctioned is made on T+1 day i.e. on the working day
following the trade day.
▪ The Reserve Bank releases a quarterly calendar of T-bill and SGSs issuances for the

Price of the Bond is Inversely proportional to the Yield of the Bond
58
▪ If market interest rate levels rise, the price of a bond falls.
Conversely, if interest rates or market yields decline, the price
of the bond rises. In other words, the yield of a bond is inversely
related to its price.
▪ This is because to if yields have risen, a purchaser will buy
previously issued bond only at discount to its price, while if
yields have fallen, the existing holder of bond carrying higher
yields will sell them only at a premium
▪ The relationship between yield to maturity and coupon rate of
bond may be stated as follows:
➢ When the market price of the bond is less than the face
value, i.e., the bond sells at a discount, YTM > coupon
yield.
➢ When the market price of the bond is more than its face
value, i.e., the bond sells at a premium, coupon yield >
YTM.
➢ When the market price of the bond is equal to its face value,
i.e., the bond sells at par, YTM = coupon yield.

More on Primary Dealer (PD) System for G-sec market in India
63
▪ The system of Primary Dealers (PDs) in the G-sec market was introduced by RBI in 1995 (& operational from
1996) to strengthen the market infrastructure and was aimed at not only a more efficient primary market but
also to put in place an improved, efficient secondary market trading system in G-secs
▪ Initially RBI set up Discount and Finance House of India (DFHI) and Securities Trading Corporation of India
(STCI) as the two initial PDs and off-loaded them later. As of end-March 2024, there were 21 PDs of which 7
were stand-alone while 14 were bank-PDs.
▪ PDs functioning include: (i) dealing and underwriting in G-secs, (ii) dealing in Interest Rate Derivatives, (iii)
providing broking services in Government securities, (iv) dealing and underwriting in Corporate / PSU / FI
bonds/ debentures, (v) lending in Call/ Notice/ Term/ Repo/ TREPS market, (vi) investment in CPs & CDs,
(vii) investment in Security Receipts issued by Securitization Companies/ Reconstruction Companies, Asset
Backed Securities (ABS), Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS).
▪ PDs ensure 100% of underwriting of a primary G-sec auction and in return are provided an incentive
structure in form of underwriting commission. The underwriting commitment is divided into two parts, viz; (i)
minimum underwriting commitment (MUC), and (ii) additional competitive underwriting (ACU). All successful
bidders in the ACU get commission as per the auction rules.
▪ Bank-PDs are required to maintain separate books of accounts for transactions relating to PD business.

Are G-secs Risk free? If not, what are the risks… (1)
68
▪ G-Secs are generally referred to as risk free instruments as sovereigns rarely default on their
payments. However, as is the case with any financial instrument, there are risks associated with
holding the G-Secs. Hence, it is important to identify and understand such risks and take
appropriate measures for mitigation of the same. The following are the major risks associated with
holding G-Secs:
▪ Market risk: Arises out of adverse movement of prices of the securities due to changes in interest
rates. This will result in valuation losses on marking to market or realizing a loss if the securities are
sold at adverse prices. Small investors, to some extent, can mitigate market risk by holding the
bonds till maturity so that they can realize the yield at which the securities were actually bought.
▪ Reinvestment risk: Cash flows on a G-Sec includes a coupon every half year and repayment of
principal at maturity. These cash flows need to be reinvested whenever they are paid. Hence there
is a risk that the investor may not be able to reinvest these proceeds at yield prevalent at the time
of making investment due to decrease in interest rates prevailing at the time of receipt of cash flows
by investors.

Are G-secs Risk free? If not, what are the risks…(2)
69
▪ Liquidity risk: Liquidity in G-Secs is referred to as the ease with which security can be bought and
sold i.e. availability of buy-sell quotes with narrow spreads. Liquidity risk refers to the inability of an
investor to liquidate (sell) his holdings due to non-availability of buyers for the security, i.e., no trading
activity in that particular security or circumstances resulting in distressed sale (selling at a much
lower price than its holding cost) causing loss to the seller. Usually, when a liquid bond of fixed
maturity is bought, its tenor gets reduced due to time decay. For example, a 10-year security will
become 8-year security after 2 years due to which it may become illiquid.
▪ The bonds also become illiquid when there are no frequent reissuances by the issuer (RBI) in those
bonds. Bonds are generally reissued till a sizeable amount becomes outstanding under that bond.
However, issuer and sovereign have to ensure that there is no excess burden on Government at the
time of maturity of the bond as very large amount maturing on a single day may affect the fiscal
position of Government. Hence, reissuances for securities are generally stopped after outstanding
under that bond touches a particular limit. Due to illiquidity, the investor may need to sell at adverse
prices in case of urgent funds requirement. However, in such cases, eligible investors can participate
in market repo and borrow the money against the collateral of such securities.

Evolution of the Global FX Code
84
▪ The FX Global Code is a comprehensive set of global principles for foreign exchange (FX) market participants that promotes integrity,
effective functioning, and transparency in the wholesale FX market.
▪ The Code was developed by a partnership between central banks and market participants from 20 jurisdictions through the Global
Foreign Exchange Committee (GFXC) under the auspices of Bank for International Settlements (BIS). GFXC was established in
May 2017 as a forum bringing together central banks and private sector participants with the aim to promote a robust, fair,
liquid, open, and appropriately transparent FX market in which a diverse set of participants, supported by resilient infrastructure, are
able to confidently and effectively transact at competitive prices that reflect available information and in a manner that conforms to
acceptable standards of behaviour.
▪ Its first version released in May 2017. It was subsequently revised in Dec. 2017; August 2018; July 2021 with its fifth version released in
Dec. 2024 that enhanced the Disclosure Cover Sheets for Liquidity Providers.
▪ The Code has been operationalized through:
➢ Statements of Commitment: Central banks maintain a public register where market participants can post their Statements of
Commitment to the Code.
➢ Internal Procedures: Market participants use the Code as a reference to develop and review their internal procedures for
conducting business in the wholesale FX markets.
➢ Local Adoption: Local FX Committees work to promote adherence to the Code and provide updates on its implementation in
different markets
➢ Global FX Code is backed by proportionality and self-assessment tools based on identified 55 Principles of the Code that
determines the proportion in which the FX market participant is able to assess his expected commitments to the Code.

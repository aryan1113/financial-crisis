What was the Global Financial Crisis (GFC)?

2

▪ GFC was a period of extreme stress in global financial markets and

banking systems between mid 2007-mid 2009 that actually exploded into
a full-scale global financial crisis in 2008.

▪

It was known as the Sub-prime crisis in its earlier phases, reflecting
credit bubble to low-income homebuyers in the US.

▪ During the GFC, a downturn in the US housing market was a catalyst for
a financial crisis that spread from the United States to the rest of the
world through linkages in the global financial system. Many banks
around the world incurred large losses and relied on government support
to avoid bankruptcy.

▪ GFC reflected a complete market failure and the international financial

system was on verge of a breakdown due to serial failure or near-failure
of several commercial and investment banks, insurance companies,
mortgage lenders and saving and loan associations.

▪

It resulted in the Great recession of 2007-09, which was the worst
economic downturn since the Great Depression of 1929-39.

▪ 1992: Congress passes legislation that requires government-sponsored mortgage giants Fannie Mae

and Freddie Mac to devote a percentage of their lending to affordable housing. This led to an
increase in the overall number of loans being pooled and securitized or sold as financial instruments
to other investors. Two years later, J.P. Morgan introduces the first credit default swap (CDS), a credit
derivative designed to act  as a kind of insurance against defaults for investors. Over the next
decade and a half, CDS become the most widely traded credit derivative product globally. The CDS
market proves to be a major source of systemic risk to the U.S. financial system during the GFC.

▪ 1995-1997: Subprime lending grew in the U.S. after the 1995 changes to the Community

Reinvestment Act. The Act was originally passed to end discrimination against low-income
borrowers. It allowed mortgage lenders to buy “subprime” securities to fulfill their affordable-housing
lending obligations.

▪ 1999: In September, Fannie Mae eased credit standards to encourage banks to extend loans to
people whose credit scores were lower than what was required for conventional loans. On 12th
November, Financial Services Modernization Act (popularly known as Gramm-Leach-Bliley
Act) substantially repealed the Banking Act of 1933 (popularly known as the Glass-Steagall Act)
that through its Section 20 provided for separation of commercial and investment banking. It paved
the way for banks holding companies to set up subsidiaries to engage several para-banking
activities, including underwriting and dealing in securities. The new Act paved the way for financial
holding company structures over which Fed was provided supervisory powers.

In 2000-2001, Fed cut its policy interest rates after the dot.com bubble burst. This led

In April, the Securities Exchange Commission (SEC) relaxed the net capital rule which
had capped broker-dealers and investment banks to a leverage (debt/ equity ratio) of
12:1. As part of the Consolidated Supervised Entities (CSE) Programme, SEC allowed
firms with over US$5 billion in assets to use their own internal mathematical models to
measure risk. This effectively lifted the traditional 12:1 leverage ratio, allowing banks to
leverage their capital an unlimited number of times.

▪ Bear Sterns, Lehman Brothers, Merrill Lynch, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley

increased the leverage to ~30 times and thus, failed to survive as independent I-banks.

▪ At the same time, SEC relieved I-banks of standard broker-dealer constraints, major
investment banks rapidly expanded their balance sheets by funding highly complex,
mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) with
short-term borrowing.

▪ What is TED Spread? It is the difference between the three-month
LIBOR and the three-month Treasury bill rate. It is is commonly
used as a measure of credit risk, as U.S. Treasury bills are seen as
risk-free. The TED spread often widens in periods of economic
crisis, as the default risk widens; the spread narrows when the
economy is more stable and defaults are less of a risk.

▪ What is the Baa Spread? It is the spread between Moodys

seasoned corporate bonds with Baa (medium rating) yield minus the
10-year Treasury constant maturity yield. It is again a measure of
credit risk

▪ What is the US VIX? It is 30-day expected volatility of the U.S.
stock market, derived from real-time, mid-quote prices of S&P
500 Index call and put options.

▪ What is MOVE? It is Merrill Lynch Option Volatility Estimate for the
bond market (equivalent of the equity market's VIX). It measures
expected, or implied, volatility in the USTstracking the prices of 1-
month options on 2-year, 5-year, 10-year, and 30-year USTs.

Source: Mishkin (2011), JEP

Securitization obfuscated risks and led to the Global Financial Crisis (GFC)

8

▪ Securitization: Hedge funds and others sold mortgage-backed

securities (MBS), collateralized Debt obligations (CDOs) and other
derivatives. A mortgage-backed security is a financial product whose
price is based on the value of the mortgages that are used
for collateral. Once you get a mortgage from a bank, the bank sells the
loan on the secondary market. The hedge fund then bundles your
mortgage with a lot of other similar mortgages.

▪ They used computer models to figure out what the bundle is worth

based on several factors. These included the monthly payments, the
total amount owed, the likelihood you will repay, and future home
prices.

▪

▪

 The hedge fund then sold the MBS to investors taking liability off-
balance sheet. It can then make new loans with the money it received.
It may still collect your payments, but it sends them along to the hedge
fund, which sends it to its investors. So, the risk was transferred from
bank to hedge fund and from hedge fund to investor.

Investors did not worry because Credit Default Swaps (CDS) offered
them insurance. But these CDS were again owned by financial
institutions like Bear sterns, Citibank and Lehman Brothers

Source: Mishkin (2011), JEP

Securitization obfuscated risks and led to the Global Financial Crisis (GFC)

▪ Another instrument used was Asset Backed Commercial Papers (ABCP). Asset-backed

commercial paper is like traditional commercial paper, in that it is issued with maturities of one
year or less (typically less than 270 days) and is highly rated. The difference between ABCP and
regular CP is that instead of being an unsecured promissory note representing an obligation of
the issuing company, ABCP is backed by securities. Therefore, the perceived quality of the ABCP
depends on the underlying securities, typically an SPV so that it is bankruptcy remote.

▪ Unlike standard commercial paper, ABCP is backed (collateralized) by a pool of assets, such as
auto loans, credit card receivables, or trade receivables and the quality of this collateral backing
was at times dubious, serving to obfuscate risks on the balance sheets.

▪ Banks typically used ABCP to move assets off their balance sheets while still maintaining liquidity
lines for their clients. ABCPs were also used by the shadow banking system (NBFIs) to fund long-
term, illiquid assets using very short-term debt. Investors frequently rolled over these securities
every few days.

▪

In Aug 2007, doubts about the quality of subprime mortgages hidden in these asset pools led
investors to stop buying ABCPs. This market freeze stranded issuers with billions in illiquid, un-
rollable debt. Fed instituted emergency lines - the Asset-Backed Commercial Paper Money
Market Mutual Fund Liquidity Facility (AMLF) and the Commercial Paper Funding Facility (CPFF)

Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP)

19

▪

In Sept. 2008, the US financial markets had freezed following the collapse of Lehman and troubles in Fannie
Mae, Freddie Mac and AIG.

▪ As situation was spiraling out of control, Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson (formerly Chairman & CEO of

Goldman Sachs) pioneered the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP). It was signed into law by President
George W. Bush on October 3, 2008, with the passage of the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act.

▪ TARP's original aim was to increase the liquidity of the money markets and secondary mortgage markets by
purchasing the mortgaged-backed securities (MBS) and through that, reduce the potential losses of the
institutions that owned them

▪

Later, TARP's aim was modified to allow the government to buy equity in banks and other financial institutions.
TARP initially gave the Treasury purchasing power of $700 bn. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and
Consumer Protection Act (Dodd-Frank Act) later reduced the authorization to $475 bn.

▪ The U.S. government bought preferred stock in eight banks: Bank of America/Merrill Lynch, Bank of New York
Mellon, Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Morgan Stanley, State Street, and Wells Fargo. The banks
were required to give the government a 5% dividend that would increase to 9% in 2013, encouraging banks to
buy back the stock within five years.

▪ From the inception till Oct 3, 2010 (the deadline for extending funds), $245 billion was used to stabilize banks,

$27 billion went to programs to increase credit availability, $80 billion went to the U.S. auto industry (specifically,
GM and Chrysler), $68 billion was used to stabilize AIG, and $46 billion went to foreclosure-prevention

▪ TARP booked US$15.3 billion in profits for taxpayers recovering $441.7 billion from $426.4 billion invested.

The Dodd-Frank Act, 2010

20

▪ The Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, commonly referred to as the Dodd–Frank Act was a
U.S. Federal law enacted under Barack Obama’s Presidency on July 21, 2010 to promote the financial stability of the U.S. by
improving accountability and transparency in the financial system, to end “too big to fail (TBTF)” to protect the American
taxpayer by ending bailouts and to protect consumers from abusive financial services practices, and for other purposes.

Some Key Provisions of the Act:

▪ Created Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) to identify fragilities & for regulatory coordination.

▪ Orderly Liquidation Authority (OLA) to wind down large, complex financial institutions.

▪ Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB): An independent agency for consumer protection by regulating consumer

financial products and services.

▪ Volcker Rule: Restricting banks from speculative proprietary trading and limiting their investments in hedge funds and private
equity funds to minimize risk to depositors and taxpayers. They were complemented by Bonus rules/ incentive compensation
rules, to regulate compensation structures so as not to incentivize excessive risk-taking.

▪ Derivatives reforms for trading them on exchanges/ electronic platforms and clearing through CCPs.

▪ Mandated regular stress tests of banks and increased capital requirements for banks

▪ Established overseeing credit rating agencies through SEC to provide meaningful and reliable ratings.

▪ Strengthen & expand the Whistleblower Program with rewards and protection to individuals exposing inappropriate behavior.

In 2018, the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act rolled back some Dodd-Frank
provisions, particularly easing regulations for smaller and mid-sized bank

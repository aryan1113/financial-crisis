Modern Monetary Theory (MMT)
18
▪ Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) advocates that governments with a fiat currency system under their control can
and should print (or create with a few keystrokes in today’s digital age) as much money as they need to spend
because they cannot go broke or be insolvent unless a political decision to do so is taken. It recommends
strengthening automatic stabilizers to control demand-pull inflation rather than relying upon discretionary tax
changes.
▪ MMT is different from the fiscal theory of the price level (FTPL). The latter is the idea that government fiscal
policy, including debt and taxes present and future, is the primary determinant of the price level or inflation as
opposed to monetary theory. FTPL represents confidence the government will not default on its debts, but rather
'inflate away' debts. FTPL believes the price level adjust so that the real value of government debt equals the
present value of taxes less spending. Inflation breaks out when people don’t expect the government to fully repay
its debts.
▪ MMT is actually playing with fire. It is a mistaken belief that a country which uses its own money, never runs of
money because they have sovereign power to issue fiat currency. However, if they do, the consequent inflation will
reduce that money to worthless paper as typically happens in hyperinflation countries where money (cash) has to
be carried on a wheelbarrows. Secondly, the notion that government can use taxes to control inflation in such a
case is quite misplaced as the whole idea behind MMT is to create additional demand. If government spends and
then taxes people to take away that additional demand, it will not generate growth that was the MMT’s objective in
the first place. Naturally, MMT has been rejected even by most liberal central banks like BoJ.

structural changes in E-commerce spending patterns.

K-shaped Recovery
25
▪ Income & wealth inequalities were on the up in most countries prior to the onset of
the pandemic. Thomas Piketty pointed out that, in 2013, the wealthiest 1% of the
population owned 25% of the total wealth in France, 30% in the U.K. 20% in
Sweden, and 32% in the U.S (not counting the wealth hidden by the rich in tax
havens).
▪ COVID-19 shock exacerbated income and wealth inequities and translated into
resilience inequalities. The poor were vulnerable in absence of buffers to cope with
income losses, especially in contact-intensive services. As access to health
services was also iniquitous, the pandemic shock had very differential effect on
different income strata

economy recover at significantly different rates. While ICT and e-commerce gained,
contact intensive services took a long time to recover from the pandemic.
▪ Financial market whipsaw accentuated inequalities & fueled K-shaped recovery on
the back of asset price boom supported by liquidity infusion. While credit spreads
for large firms quickly returned to pre-pandemic levels with infusion of liquidity, the
small firms funding costs remained high. HNI’s led IPO boom through leveraged

Rebuilding Social Contracts
28
▪ In many countries, especially EMs, social contracts are an important means of offering
insurance against shocks (medical exigencies, unemployment, income losses, law and order,
etc.). COVID-19 tested the resilience of these contracts as the disease was contagious and
virus spread was aerial and contact intensive. Yet, social contracts were important means in
enforcing in localized quarantines and in affording basic food supplies to quarantined people.
Local bodies interacted with societies to enforce these quarantines and localized lockdowns.
▪ Market mechanisms were affected during the pandemic. Authoritarian governments can use
outright force to limit negative externalities. Open societies have to rely on communication
and persuasion. Government interventions had to limit individual freedom, especially mobility,
closure of contact intensive public and private services such as swimming pools and testing
& vaccine hesitancy. Social norms reinforced compliances.
▪ One lesson from COVID-19 is that information flows should be kept unimpeded for social
contracts to work effectively. A second lesson is to let institutions, especially scientific
institutions, work to strategize responses. as national lockdowns were particularly costly. A
third lesson is to decentralize decision-making in a pandemic where localized approaches
worked far better than nationalized lockdowns for the life vs livelihood trade offs
(misplaced?). These lessons along with fighting inequality can help rebuild effective social
contracts.

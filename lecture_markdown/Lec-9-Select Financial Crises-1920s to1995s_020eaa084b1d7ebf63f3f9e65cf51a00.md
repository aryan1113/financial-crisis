11
▪ The routes of the crisis lay in the two large oil price shocks in the 1970s that caused
widening of current account deficits in many Latin American (LatAm) countries. Oil-

The Policies of heterodox shocks…(1)
What were these policies?
13
▪ Stabilization programs introduced by Argentina, Bolivia and Brazil (also in Israel) between July 1985
and March 1986, when hyperinflation was an imminent threat to each of these economies, so that a
primary policy aim had to be the attainment of a quick and drastic reduction in inflation.
▪ However, inflation in Latin America was “inertial’ (implying automatic move of the inflation in the same
direction) in characteristic, meaning that with high level of indexations in wage and other contracts
(wages were fully indexed in many cases, implying nominal wages automatically increased in line with
inflation rate to compensate labour fully)
▪ They introduced heterodox shocks. First, in an effort to influence expectations they created major
discontinuities in the trends of the main macroeconomic variables, rather than following the gradual
adjustment paths of traditional stabilization packages. For this reason, they have usually been referred
to as “shock” programs. Second, in most cases, they included a number of incomes-policy elements,
such as wage and price freezes, together with exchange rate pegging and deindexation measures.
These components have been characterized as “heterodox,” as compared with the “orthodox”
emphasis on demand management through fiscal and monetary instruments.
▪ However, in practice, traditional “fundamental” policies, mainly in the monetary and fiscal areas,
formed the bulk of each program. In fact, the designers of the shock programs considered
the combination of heterodox and fundamental (demand-management) policies crucial to the success
of the programs.

Often, an important components of these heterodox shock policies was to replace one currency with another. Since 1942,
Brazil replaced currencies seven times (had eight different currencies, starting with the Cruzeiro).
▪ The Cruzado Plan (1986): Launched by President José Sarney, it replaced the cruzeiro with the cruzado (at 1,000
cruzeiros = 1 cruzado). It was imposed with immediate general price and wage freeze, & de-indexing the economy.
➢ The Outcome: The plan created a temporary surge in consumer purchasing power, causing widespread shortages and
a massive wage-price spiral of demand-induced inflation. By late 1986, the plan collapsed. Inflation rebounded.
▪ The Bresser and Summer Plans (1987 & 1989): As the inflation had re-accelerated, the government introduced the
Bresser Plan (1987) and the Summer Plan (1989), both featuring new price freezes and temporary de-indexation.
➢ The Outcome: The heterodox measure offered only temporary relief. Price controls quickly became unsustainable as
firms anticipated future freezes and created supply distortions.
▪ The Collor Plans (1989-91): President Fernando Collor de Mello introduced sweeping heterodox measures
accompanied by aggressive orthodox policies. The cruzado novo was briefly introduced exchanging 1000 cruzerio=1
cruzado novo but it proved to be short-lived only for the period 15th Jan, 1989-15th Mar 1990. The currency was
later renamed cruzeiro. The government also shockingly confiscated large fractions of private savings and checking
accounts to restrict liquidity.
➢ The Outcome: The asset seizures caused loss of consumer & business confidence resulting in a deep economic
contraction and widespread hardship, yet failed to sustainably break the country's inflationary inertia.

▪ The Plano Real (1994): Was designed by a team of economists including President Fernando Henrique Cardoso himself.
This program departed from prior heterodox shocks by focusing on de-indexing rather than enforcing immediate price
freezes. In March 1994, the government introduced the Unidade Real de Valor (URV) as a transitional unit of account tied to
the U.S. dollar. Prices, wages, and other contracts were slowly converted into URVs, which stabilized inflation expectations.
This allowed Brazilians to recalibrate contracts and compare prices in a stable unit while still transacting in the old currency,
the cruzeiro real, which at that point had circulated for only 10 months. On July 1, 1994, the government officially introduced
the Brazilian Real (BRL), replacing the cruzeiro real at parity with the URV. Because the transition period already de-
indexed the economy, the introduction of the Real did not trigger the same shock-induced demand or supply crunches seen in
1986.
➢ Outcome: Supported by tight monetary policies and fiscal adjustments, it succeeded in finally taming Brazil’s chronic
hyperinflation. From over 2,000% annual inflation rate dropped to single digits in 1996. But the gains of this successful
disinflation were frittered away by lack of fiscal discipline. Long-term debt rose again causing a currency crisis in Brazil in
1999. The Real initially appreciated against the USD led by large capital inflows in late 1994 and 1995. It then began a
gradual depreciation, culminating in the Jan. 1999 free fall, when the real suffered a maxi-devaluation amid wide fluctuations.
The period of a quasi-fixed exchange rate during 1994-1999, was succeeded by Brazil central bank president Armino Fraga
adopting inflation-targeting, which effectively meant that the fixed-exchange period was over. However, the currency was
never freely floating but a "dirty" float, with frequent central bank interventions to manipulate its dollar price. Since Brazil’s
macroeconomic stabilization program was implemented in 1994, the percentage of Brazilians living in poverty has decreased
and the distribution of income has registered a small improvement. But poverty and income inequality remain severe.

▪ The Samba effect (1999): nickname for the currency crisis in Brazil; Real depreciated by 35%. Three main causes:
▪ (1) Dirty Float: While Brazil successfully ended hyperinflation by pegging the new currency (the real) to the US dollar.as part of the
Plano Real, it continued to try keeping new currency stable and the exchange rate regime worked like a Dirty Float. Therefore, real
became overvalued over time, causing cheap imports to flood the country and leading to massive trade deficits;
▪ (2) Contagion: Spillovers from global shocks hit the Brazilian financial markets. The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis increased country
risk premia for EMEs steeply widening spreads. As Asian crisis sparked global slowdown, demand for oil fell severely effecting the
fiscal position of Russia, which is a major oil exporter. Public Debt strains magnified at a time when political crisis brewed as in 23rd
March 1998, President Boris Yeltsin suddenly dismissed PM Viktor Chernomyrdin & his entire cabinet & named 35-year old Energy
Minister Sergei Kiriyenko as acting PM. The Russian GKO crisis that caused Russian government to default on its sovereign debt and
devalue Ruble on 17th August 1988 had ripple effects on many EMEs, including Brazil; GKOs were short-term, zero-coupon
Russian T-bills on which Russia government was offering super-normal yields
▪ (3) High Interest Rates: To defend Ruble and stem the capital flight, Brazil's central bank resorted to interest rate hikes as a line of
defence raising short-term rates to extremely high levels. This made it very expensive for the government to pay off its debt and
sparked fears of a default. The Collapse followed.
Conclusion: Policies of heterodox shocks have succeeded on some occasions but miserably failed on others; so difficult to make a virtue
of these policies. Direct control or freezes of prices have at times delivered reduced inflation but are not costless as markets don’t clear,
leading to real output losses and inefficient allocation of resources. It is best to use heterodox policies sparingly when alternative policies
to dampen inflation expectations are not working & complement them with a strong structural adjustment programme.

The European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) Crisis… (1)
18
▪ There have been on and off formal exchange rate arrangements made between European countries
between “ins” (those who want integration/ union) and “outs” (those who want to stay out of such
arrangements.
▪ The European Exchange Rate Mechanism (now known as ERM I) was an arrangement established by
the European Community (EC) in 1979 as a system of adjustable pegs that lasted till 1st Jan. 1999 when
the single currency euro was launched. The system also introduced European Currency Unit (ECU) as a
composite "artificial" currency based on a basket of European Union (EU) member currencies, weighted
according to each country’s share of EU output. The ECU started off with nine currencies and by late 1989
through the end of 1999, the basket held 12 currencies. The currencies were the Belgian franc, German
mark, Danish krone, Spanish peseta, French franc, British Pound, Greek drachma, Irish pound, Italian lira,
Luxembourg franc, Dutch guilder, and Portuguese escudo.
▪ While it initially operated to reduce exchange rate variability in the floating rate era, the foundational
blueprint for a three-stage transition to euro was laid by the Delors Report in 1989 named after EC
President Jacques Delors, that outlined a step-by-step transition to a single currency. Stage-I from July
1990 to 1993 aimed at market integration through complete liberalization of capital movements amongst
EEC states. Stage-II from 1994 to 1998 aimed at economic and institutional convergence by enhancing
independence of the European National Central Banks, enhancing fiscal discipline and setting up
European Monetary Institute (EMI) as a precursor to European System of Central Banks (ESCB). Stage-III
was to launch euro as a single currency in the euro area comprising of those countries who met the
Convergence Criteria by irrevocably fixing the exchange rates of these countries with the euro.

The European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) Crisis… (2)
20
The Convergence Criteria was laid down in the Maastrict Treaty of 1991.
▪ Low inflation. The average inflation rate observed during a one-year period before a
country is examined for admission to the single currency must not be more than 1.5% higher
than the average of the three best performing Member States in terms of price stability.
▪ Sound public finances. The government deficit must not exceed 3% of gross domestic
product (GDP ) and the public debt must not exceed 60 % of GDP, unless the ratio is
sufficiently diminishing and approaching the reference value at a satisfactory pace. This
latter criterion is therefore more flexible with greater scope for discretion.
▪ Stable exchange rates. Candidate countries must have observed the normal fluctuation
margins provided for by the exchange rate mechanism of the European Monetary
System for at least two years, without devaluing their currency against that of any other
Member State.
▪ Low interest rates. During the year preceding the examination, the average long-term
interest rate must not be more than 2% above that of the three best performing Member
States in terms of price stability.

▪ The European Exchange Rate Mechanism II (ERM II) was established as part of the Maastricht Treaty in 1999 to
guide the entry into European Monetary Union (EMU) with single currency euro for those who may wish to join it
in future (after the launch of euro). It seeks to minimizes their currency volatility and serves as a mandatory
stepping stone for EU member aiming to officially adopt the euro as their national currency. Successful
participation in ERM II is part of the ‘convergence criteria’ set for entry into EMU.
▪ Central parity: When a country joins ERM-II, its central bank and the ECB agree on a central exchange rate
against the euro. Central parities under ERM-II can be adjusted to prevent significant misalignments provided all
parties agree in a confidential procedure.
▪ Fluctuation Band: The national currency is then allowed to fluctuate within ± 15% around this central parity.
▪ Intervention: If the currency drifts too close to its upper or lower limits, the participating central banks intervene
(e.g., by buying or selling the currency) to stabilize it within the allowed range. The commitment of the ECB to
intervene in the FX market to support a particular non-euro currency is agreed to be limited under ERM-II.
▪ Countries under ERM II: Set up in 1997, the Danish krone was the only currency participating in ERM II for
several years. After 2004 enlargement of the EU, the currencies of the Baltic countries, Cyprus, Malta, Slovakia
and Slovenia all entered the mechanism. Croatian kuna and Bulgarian lev entered ERM II in 2020 & adopted euro
in 2023 & 2026, respectively. Denmark had joined ERM-II in 1999 but opted out due to “No” referendum on
Maastricht Treaty & later on euro. But it remains part of ERM II & ties itself to +/-2.25% fluctuation band to euro.

The Mexican Peso (Tequila) crisis of 1994-95
24
▪ The Mexican peso crisis was a currency crisis sparked by the Mexican
government's sudden devaluation of the Mexican peso against US dollar in
December 1994. The crisis was first of its kind ignited by capital flight

Lessons from the Tequila crisis of 1994-95

▪ In response, the Mexican central bank intervened in the foreign exchange markets to
maintain the Mexican peso’s peg to the U.S. dollar by issuing more Tesabonos & Cetes
(bills). The peso's strength caused demand for imports to increase in Mexico, resulting in a
trade deficit. Speculators recognized an overvalued peso and capital began flowing out of
Mexico to the United States, increasing downward market pressure on the peso. Under
election pressures, Mexico purchased its own treasury securities to maintain its money
supply and avert rising interest rates, drawing down the bank's dollar reserves. Supporting
the money supply by buying more dollar-denominated debt while simultaneously honoring
such debt depleted the bank's reserves by the end of 1994.
